Case File #006

The USS Lincoln Record: Hung Cao at His Navy Secretary Hearing

On Tuesday, Nov. 17, 2026, acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao appeared before the Senate Armed Services Committee for his confirmation hearing. The sharpest exchanges were about the aircraft carrier USS Abraham Lincoln and its strike group, and how their long deployment had been described: in an Aug. 14 statement on the Navy's website ("a small number of mental health cases were treated with no loss of life"), in a September letter to Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand, as reported by The Maritime Executive ("a total of eight suicide attempts across the strike group", no deaths), and at the hearing ("these are eight mitigations we're able to mitigate"). The August and September descriptions are not the same measure: one spoke of the carrier, the other counted the whole strike group over a longer period. Senators also asked about conditions, costs and what Congress is told. Because he left active duty in October 2021, federal law bars his appointment before October 2028 unless Congress passes a waiver. As of Nov. 18, we found no announced date for a committee vote or a waiver. The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    The Nominee

    According to his official Navy biography, Hung Cao came to the United States as a refugee from Vietnam in 1975, enlisted in the Navy in 1989, graduated from the Naval Academy and served as a Navy diver and explosive ordnance disposal officer, with deployments to Iraq, Afghanistan and Somalia. He retired from active duty in October 2021 as a captain. He was confirmed as Under Secretary of the Navy and became acting secretary on April 22, 2026. At the hearing, according to The Hill, he named three priorities: "Take care of our sailors and marines, advance shipbuilding initiatives and defend our homeland." The committee's chairman, Republican Sen. Roger Wicker of Mississippi, opened the hearing in his support.

  2. Exhibit B

    The August Statement

    The USS Abraham Lincoln left San Diego in November 2025, according to Stars and Stripes, and spent much of its deployment supporting the war with Iran and the blockade of Iranian ports. In August, Stars and Stripes reported that sailors and their families had raised mental health concerns at town hall meetings with senior officials; wider coverage and online claims described food and water shortages and difficult living conditions. On Aug. 14, the acting secretary answered in a statement on the Navy's website: "the media are trying to paint our warriors as victims. Not only is that dishonest but it takes away the focus on the enemy..." He listed 266 days deployed and 200 in a combat zone, and wrote that "a small number of mental health cases were treated with no loss of life" and that meal plans were adjusted "without a single meal being missed." That week, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said the reports were "completely misrepresented," and President Trump, asked whether the Lincoln had been deployed too long, said, "Not nearly long enough."

  3. Exhibit C

    The September Letter

    In September, the acting secretary wrote to Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand, a New York Democrat. According to The Maritime Executive, which reported the letter on Sept. 23, he wrote: "Since the beginning of Lincoln's deployment, there have been a total of eight suicide attempts across the strike group," meaning the carrier and the ships sailing with it, and noted there had been no deaths. The same report says the letter put physical illness, injury and mishap rates within normal limits for a carrier deployment, and that in August the top U.S. commander in the Middle East had said the Lincoln had "among the lowest number of cases related to mental health." The letter itself was not available to this file. The August and September descriptions are not the same measure: one spoke of the Lincoln, the other counted the whole strike group over a longer period. Both say no one died.

  4. Exhibit D

    The Hearing

    On Nov. 17, according to The Hill, the acting secretary confirmed he had called the news reports "dishonest," adding that sailors he spoke with on board felt the same way. Asked by Sen. Gillibrand about the eight attempts, he said: "these are eight mitigations we're able to mitigate." She replied: "Please don't call attempted suicide mitigations. That is such an insult." According to CNBC, he used the word because personnel had intervened, and he defended the Navy's mental health support. The two disagreed about whether one sailor's going overboard was an attempt; the record available to this file does not settle it. He told Sen. Richard Blumenthal that he cares "greatly" about the sailors and does not control how long deployments last. Sen. Tammy Duckworth pressed him on living conditions; he said he had gone out to the ships to talk to sailors personally. Sen. Mark Kelly said his office was hearing concerns about other ships, the USS George Washington and the Boxer Amphibious Ready Group, and cited one parent's account that their child lost more than 45 pounds in six months; according to CNBC, the acting secretary said ships had never run out of food.

  5. Exhibit E

    The Cost at Sea

    Sen. Elizabeth Warren asked the daily cost of the U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports. The acting secretary first said he did not know, then appeared to agree that an independent estimate she cited, about $32.5 million a day, sounded about right; the figure covers the blockade, not the whole war. Asked by Sen. Mazie Hirono whether the war was straining the Navy, he said: "it is stressing our naval resources," and argued that it had "really set China back on their heels," which Sen. Hirono disputed. According to CNBC, he said the Navy changed the condition codes on munitions classified as expired so they could be used, and that Tomahawk production is "well above 60 every month." Sen. Jack Reed noted that the United States had no forward-deployed carrier in the Pacific.

  6. Exhibit F

    What Congress Is Told

    Sen. Blumenthal said the nominee had failed to give Congress "a full and transparent accounting" of the toll on sailors. Sen. Reed faulted him for not notifying Congress of the removal of the USS South Dakota's commanding officer, executive officer and chief of the boat; the acting secretary said the matter was under investigation and he wanted to protect the officers' privacy, and, asked by Sen. Tim Kaine why Congress was not told, said, according to The Hill, "there's no excuse, senator." Asked by Sen. Jeanne Shaheen about a public shipyard's budget shortfall, he said he had not brought back the answers she asked for in May: "I failed you." Republican Sen. Mike Rounds said Democrats had been left out of classified briefings and asked for a bipartisan commitment, which the nominee gave.

  7. Exhibit G

    The Law

    Under 10 U.S.C. § 8013(a)(2), "A person may not be appointed as Secretary of the Navy within seven years after relief from active duty as a commissioned officer of a regular component of an armed force." The acting secretary retired from active duty in October 2021, so seven years would be October 2028. To be appointed now, he needs a waiver passed by both the House and the Senate. The closest precedents involve the Secretary of Defense: according to Military Times, Congress granted such a waiver to George Marshall, then to Jim Mattis in 2017 and Lloyd Austin in 2021; later in 2021 Congress changed the rule for service secretaries to seven years (Pub. L. 117-81). In the Senate, a waiver bill would generally need 60 votes to move forward. Democrats are set to hold majorities in both chambers when the new Congress begins in January.

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.

Open questions

  1. Why did the attempts happen? The record does not establish a cause.
  2. Will the committee vote, and will Congress pass a waiver?
  3. Will the Navy give Congress the full accounting senators asked for?
