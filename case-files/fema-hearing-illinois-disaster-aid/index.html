Case File #014

FEMA Chief Asked Why Illinois Was Denied: "At His Discretion"

On Thursday, Nov. 19, 2026, the House Homeland Security Committee's Subcommittee on Emergency Management and Technology held a hearing titled "Beyond the Storm: Assessing FEMA's Mission Readiness." The only witness was FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton, who led the agency in an acting role in early 2025, was removed that May, and was confirmed by the Senate on Aug. 7, 2026. Rep. Delia Ramirez (D-Ill.) asked him why Illinois was denied a major disaster declaration. He answered that Stafford Act declarations "are at his discretion," meaning the president's. The dollar figures she cited, $27.6 million in costs against FEMA's $24.9 million mark, belong to Illinois' request after its August 2026 storm; the counties she named belong to an earlier storm, in August 2025. FEMA denied the 2026 request in a letter dated Oct. 7; the state says it gave no specific reason. Illinois filed its appeal on Nov. 5, and as of Nov. 25 we found no decision. The law says the president "may declare" a major disaster. FEMA's regulation uses a per-resident figure, $1.94 for disasters starting on or after Oct. 1, 2025, as an "indicator" that a disaster "might warrant" federal help, alongside other factors. The hearing also covered the February snowstorm requests from New York and New Jersey, Hamilton's view that the federal government has too often been treated as a default financial backstop, the BRIC program and a federal court ruling, the Government Accountability Office's counts of FEMA departures, flood insurance premiums, and two things that run out on Dec. 11 unless Congress acts: government funding and the flood insurance program's authority to write new policies. This file does not rule on who is right: it sets out what was said, what the documents say, and what is still open.

Subject
The Nov. 19, 2026, hearing of the House Homeland Security Committee's Subcommittee on Emergency Management and Technology, read from the official recording of the Homeland Security Committee, with each member's and the witness's words in their own voices, set beside Illinois' appeal letter, the Stafford Act, FEMA's regulations, the Federal Register notice of the per-resident figure, and reports from the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service. The only witness was FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton. This file does not rule on whether any state should have received aid or who is responsible. The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    Illinois

    Rep. Delia Ramirez (D-Ill.) told the subcommittee: "When my state, Illinois, asked for federal assistance from FEMA after terrible summer storms we were denied not once, but actually three times." The state's records describe two different storms. The first was in August 2025, in Boone, Cook, Kane, McHenry and Will counties. Illinois says its request was denied that fall, and its appeal was denied in a letter dated Feb. 7, 2026; the state said that denial covered aid to individual residents. The second was in August 2026, in eight counties from Cook and Will to Champaign and Fulton. That is the storm with the dollar figures: the state's appeal letter lists $27.6 million in eligible public costs, against a FEMA mark of $24.9 million. FEMA denied that request in a letter dated Oct. 7, and according to the state the letter gave no specific reason. So the figures belong to the 2026 storm. Two denials on the 2025 storm and one on the 2026 storm may be how she counted three; the state's paperwork also lists a July 2025 flood among its denied requests, so the count depends on how the storms are grouped. Rep. Ramirez did not say how she counted. She also said Illinois appealed and was denied again; that is true of the 2025 storm. On the 2026 storm, Illinois filed its appeal on Nov. 5 and says FEMA has since validated $43 million in eligible costs. Capitol News Illinois reports that 18 state lawmakers sent statements supporting the appeal, 12 of them Republicans. As of Nov. 25, 2026, we found no decision. She then asked about Indiana, hit by the same storm system in August 2026, which received a major disaster declaration on Aug. 25, 2026. Capitol News Illinois reports that Indiana's governor asked for emergency aid right away, while Illinois filed its request in mid-September. When Illinois first applied, four of its eight counties, including Cook, were under their own county marks; the state's appeal says six of the eight are now over. FEMA has not said whether any of that played a part. Administrator Hamilton began to list kinds of damage; Rep. Ramirez cut in before he said which, if any, Illinois had failed to show, and told him he did not need to answer. At the start of her time she had given approval rates by party; we could not find the source of those rates and do not repeat them. Later, answering Rep. Vince Fong (R-Calif.), Hamilton came back to the subject: "while a declaration threshold may or may not be achieved, it doesn't lessen the intensity of what individual survivors are going through..."

  2. Exhibit B

    The Rule

    The Stafford Act (42 U.S.C. 5170) says that on a governor's request, "the President may declare" a major disaster. May, not must. FEMA's regulation (44 CFR 206.48) tells FEMA to compare the cost with the state's population. For Illinois' 2026 storm the figure was $1.94 per resident, which comes to about $24.9 million. The regulation calls it an "indicator" that a disaster "might warrant" federal help, and it lists other factors, including local damage, insurance and other recent disasters. So passing the mark does not guarantee aid, but the regulation calls these the primary factors in FEMA's recommendation to the president. Earlier in the hearing, Rep. Eli Crane (R-Ariz.) had asked Hamilton what happens after the damage is counted; Hamilton said FEMA packages the facts and "we await the president's decision." What FEMA recommended on Illinois was not said at the hearing. FEMA told Capitol News Illinois in October 2026 that it reviews each request closely under the law and its regulations.

  3. Exhibit C

    The Snow

    Rep. Tim Kennedy (D-N.Y.), the subcommittee's ranking member, raised a blizzard in February 2026, named New York and New Jersey, and asked why the aid was withheld. Hamilton said states that see repeated and consistent snowstorms should be expected to provide their own funding and resources. FEMA's regulation (44 CFR 206.227) says snow declarations are made only for "record or near record" snowstorms; New York's appeal, dated July 31, 2026, says four of its counties met that record-snow test. Rep. Kennedy also gave dollar figures that we could not match to New York's filing. The filing puts New York's mark at $39.2 million, says $54.6 million in damage had been validated when the state asked, and says the state and local governments counted $168 million in all. The first denial is dated July 2, 2026, before Hamilton was confirmed. New York's governor said FEMA denied the appeal on Sept. 2, 2026, after Hamilton took office. New York's appeal letter says FEMA validated more than $20 million in damage to the Long Island Power Authority from trees falling on power lines, which differs from how Hamilton described the damage (he said there was "no categorical damage to infrastructure"); we found no document for his statement that 95% of the costs requested were for snowplows and other snow removal.

  4. Exhibit D

    Who Should Pay

    In his opening statement Hamilton said "the federal government has too often been treated as a default financial backstop" for incidents that at times are best managed at the state, local, tribal or territorial level. His written testimony says this has delayed aid, raised costs to taxpayers and discouraged states from preparing. A council the president set up reported in May 2026. One option it gave was to raise the per-resident figure from $1.94 to $2.99; by its own analysis, 29% of the disasters declared from 2012 to 2025 would not have met that mark. This file does not rule on the proposal. At the hearing, Rep. Crane said that a second mark, the one for counties, is already too hard for some places: counties in his district are mostly federal land, which cannot be taxed. He said he has introduced a bill to cut the county mark in half for places like that.

  5. Exhibit E

    BRIC

    BRIC gives communities money to prepare before the next disaster. In his opening, Rep. Kennedy told Hamilton: "You unlawfully shut down the Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities BRIC program." The record comes from the Congressional Research Service. In April 2025, while Hamilton was acting head, FEMA announced it was ending the program and said about $882 million would go back to the Treasury or be reassigned by Congress. Twenty states sued. That July, after he had left, FEMA told the court it had not ended the program. In December 2025 a federal judge ruled that the administration had unlawfully terminated BRIC, and in March 2026 the judge ordered FEMA to follow that ruling. FEMA then offered $1 billion in new grants. The ruling did not require it to fund any particular project. That is the record as of the research service's report at the end of June 2026. Answering Rep. Fong at the hearing, Hamilton described BRIC as a very unique program that provides additional mitigation dollars.

  6. Exhibit F

    The People and the Plan

    In August 2026, the Government Accountability Office reported that FEMA rescinded its strategic plan in 2025. Resident Commissioner Pablo José Hernández (D-P.R.) asked about the new one; Hamilton described its three aims and, asked whether the plan is public, said it is not yet, that FEMA is working to release it "in the near future," and that he did not have a date. The watchdog counted 4,321 departures in the budget year that ended in September 2025, and 2,545 more by mid-May 2026. FEMA hired about 3,000 people over that stretch, nearly all of them in the first year. When Mr. Hernández asked how many people FEMA needs, Hamilton said that Congress sets limits through appropriations, gave a figure for permanent staff that we could not confirm, and said a workforce analysis is underway; the watchdog's count for CORE employees, disaster staff on multi-year terms, was about 9,800 in April 2026. Mr. Hernández then asked whether FEMA has the people it needs if another Hurricane Maria hits; Hamilton answered, "Yes, I believe in my staff." The watchdog asked FEMA to issue a strategic plan and work out how many people it needs; it says the Department of Homeland Security, FEMA's parent department, agreed. There was also credit at the hearing: Rep. Troy Carter (D-La.) thanked Hamilton for extending deadlines on Hurricane Katrina projects, Rep. Fong thanked him for $57 million in reimbursements, and Hamilton pointed to a test program in Indiana.

  7. Exhibit G

    Flood Insurance

    FEMA runs the National Flood Insurance Program. In 2021 it began setting prices a new way, called Risk Rating 2.0. Rep. Carter said families have opened their flood insurance bills with more questions than answers, and put it in terms of a house with no mortgage. Hamilton said FEMA runs the program on behalf of Congress "where there is a marketplace gap" and that there were more reforms than he could go into, and offered to meet with Rep. Carter's team. He gave no specifics on premiums. We found no official count of how many policyholders' premiums have risen, so this file does not repeat the percentages given at the hearing.

Open questions

  1. Will FEMA decide Illinois' appeal on the August 2026 storm? Illinois filed it on Nov. 5, 2026; as of Nov. 25, 2026, we found no decision.
  2. Will Congress act by Dec. 11, 2026, on government funding and the flood insurance program's authority to write new policies? Existing policies would stay in force until their own end dates.
  3. Will FEMA publish its strategic plan, and say how many people it needs? Hamilton gave no date and no number at the hearing; he said a workforce analysis is underway.
  4. Will the hearing record be published? The chairman said it would stay open for 10 days for written questions.
  5. Should passing the mark mean a state gets help, or should the president decide each time?
