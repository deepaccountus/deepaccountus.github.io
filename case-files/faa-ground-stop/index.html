Case File #005

What Stopped U.S. Flights? Four Explanations in Four Days

On Friday, Nov. 13, 2026, at about 3:30 p.m. Eastern, the FAA's air traffic network became unstable, and the agency stopped departures across the United States for more than four hours. About 3,500 aircraft in the air landed safely. Over the next four days the cause was described four ways: old equipment overloaded, with the new equipment holding up (Secretary Duffy, Friday); new equipment overwhelming the older system (Secretary Mullin, Friday); by the FAA's initial investigation, a new router at a remote site flooding an old network (Secretary Duffy on Saturday, the FAA in writing on Sunday); and old equipment plus copper-to-fiber work under the previous administration (President Trump, Monday). The first three are not the same explanation, but they can partly fit together; the record does not yet show which work the president is referring to. The FAA's review of the root cause is still open.

The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    The Stop

    The FAA's own account, published on Sunday, Nov. 15, gives the timeline. At about 3:30 p.m. Eastern on Friday, the agency "experienced a technical problem." Instability began at 17 of its 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers and spread into several approach facilities. It was not a total blackout: the FAA says some radio frequencies and radar feeds kept working, so controllers could still talk to and track aircraft, and medical, law enforcement and military flights kept moving. About 3,500 aircraft in the air landed safely. At 8:15 p.m. the system was stable and flights resumed. The FAA says operators canceled about 12,462 flights on Friday, including 9,082 by major airlines, and adds that not all of those cancellations were directly caused by the ground stop.

  2. Exhibit B

    Friday Night

    At about 8:30 p.m., Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy briefed reporters at the White House. Secretary Duffy said the system is old and that old equipment was overloaded: "Our switches, our routers actually got overloaded with data." He added that "our new IP switches did not go down. It's the old hardware that went down." Asked about a cyberattack, Secretary Mullin said there was zero evidence of one, though nothing was ruled out. Later in the briefing he said that, as it appeared at that point, the FAA had been putting in new equipment and new technology, and that it "was overwhelming the older system." Both can be partly true: new parts can work perfectly and still send an old network more than it can handle. Secretary Duffy said that night that the details were still being worked out, and that the FAA's new data tool, SMART, "isn't plugged in" to the air traffic control system.

  3. Exhibit C

    The Written Account

    On Saturday, Nov. 14, Secretary Duffy pointed to a router installed by the contractor that runs the FAA's telecommunications network, L3Harris. He said the cause was either "contractor error or a faulty piece of equipment," and that "we haven't determined what the fault was." On Sunday, the FAA put the account in writing: its legacy equipment "became unstable when data began flooding the FAA FTI network, at a rate of 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds." The initial investigation points to two potential locations: a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, and a radio communications site in El Dorado, Arkansas, where L3Harris had been deploying a new router. L3Harris blocked the two locations and the network stabilized. The FAA disabled the same function on more than 60 other Juniper routers and paused installing them, and said it would reevaluate doing such work in the daytime. Asked whether its upgrades caused this, the FAA answered no: the cause was the legacy system becoming unstable. L3Harris said on Nov. 14 that the conversion from copper to fiber is more than 70% complete and that it is supporting the FAA's investigation. The FAA says it and L3Harris continue to investigate the root cause.

  4. Exhibit D

    The Money

    Congress provided $12.5 billion for air traffic control in 2025, in the law known as the One Big Beautiful Bill; the FAA's Sunday document calls it a down payment and uses the law's newer name, the Working Families Tax Cuts. The administration has asked for about $30 billion more, a request first made in September 2026. On Friday night, Secretary Duffy said: "the President's asked for $30 billion. I want $50 billion." On Sunday morning, President Trump wrote on Truth Social that "the Biden Administration was an FAA disaster," and that the work "should be done all at once, not in drips and drabs. Congress must approve."

  5. Exhibit E

    The Fourth Explanation

    On Monday, Nov. 16, before leaving for Asia, President Trump was asked what happened on Friday. He said: "We have old equipment, broken equipment," and that "the Biden administration hooked up a lot of copper into glass," meaning copper connected to fiber, and that money was wasted. The FAA's own document says this type of copper-to-fiber work "has been going on for years," and the router it identifies was, by Secretary Duffy's account, being installed on Friday, under the current administration. The record available to this file does not show which part of the old network the president is referring to, or when that work was done.

  6. Exhibit F

    Congress

    Senate Majority Leader John Thune, a Republican, said on Nov. 16 that about $8 billion of the $12.5 billion has not been spent, and that "there's some explanations that have to be given as to why that number is where it is." Sen. John Cornyn, a Texas Republican, said, as reported by CNN: "I think it's highly unlikely that Congress is just going to write a check for $50 billion just because he says so." Sen. Susan Collins, a Maine Republican and senior appropriator, also quoted by CNN, said she does not "see the justification yet for that much." The Department of Transportation says the $12.5 billion is allocated to specific projects and cannot be moved to a new telecommunications network. On Nov. 17, according to ABC News, the department formally submitted a $50.1 billion request, and the 13 Democrats on the Senate Commerce Committee asked for "clear, fact-based answers about the root cause of this disruption and why existing safeguards failed to prevent it," a pause on installing the new routers until further testing, and technical briefings from the FAA, L3Harris and Juniper. That afternoon, ABC reported, the FAA announced it was halting installation of those routers.

  7. Exhibit G

    The Last Time

    On Jan. 11, 2023, the FAA paused all domestic departures after an overnight failure of a different system, the one that sends safety notices to pilots, known as NOTAM. At 8:50 a.m. Eastern, the FAA announced the ground stop had been lifted. That evening it traced the outage to "a damaged database file" and said there was no evidence of a cyberattack. Eight days later, in a still-preliminary review, it said contract personnel had "unintentionally deleted files" while working to synchronize the main database with a backup. Different systems, different causes; but the record follows a similar pattern: work by contract personnel on an FAA system, a first explanation within hours, and a more specific one days later.

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.

Open questions

  1. Was it a faulty router, or the way it was installed?
  2. Why did one or two remote sites affect most of the country?
  3. Will the money, $30 billion or $50 billion, be approved, and with what conditions?
