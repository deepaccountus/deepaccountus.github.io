Case File #017

VA Psychedelic Therapy: Est. 180,000 Eligible, 10 Sites Not Announced

On Wednesday, Nov. 18, 2026, the House Veterans' Affairs Subcommittee on Health, chaired by Rep. Mariannette Miller-Meeks (R-Iowa), heard from the Department of Veterans Affairs and four outside experts on psychedelic-assisted therapy for veterans. Neither MDMA nor psilocybin is approved by the FDA for any use, and federal rules list both in Schedule I. Andrew Gradison, a senior advisor at the Veterans Health Administration, said about 6.4 million veterans actively use VA care and roughly 2.1 million have been diagnosed with treatment-resistant depression, major depressive disorder or post-traumatic stress disorder; applying study criteria, he said roughly 180,000 might be eligible. He told Rep. Maggie Goodlander (D-N.H.) that is his estimate, not a firm number; it is not in his written testimony. The VA's written testimony says it has identified ten facilities to serve as early implementers, educators or sites-in-training. The VA has not named them in public. At the hearing it agreed to give the committee the list and a staffing plan with costs, and Goodlander asked for them within 30 days. The VA said its fiscal year 2027 budget did not include a request for specific funding for this work. Members raised concerns about private clinics and about VA staffing. As of Nov. 28, we found no FDA approval of a classic psychedelic, and neither the site list nor the staffing plan has been made public.

Subject
The Nov. 18, 2026, hearing of the Subcommittee on Health of the House Committee on Veterans' Affairs, "Psychedelic-Assisted Therapies for Veterans: The Path Forward," read from the committee's official recording, with each member's and witness's words in their own voices, set beside the VA's written testimony, Executive Order 14401, the VA's own announcements and the federal drug schedules. The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    The count

    Subcommittee Chair Mariannette Miller-Meeks, a physician and an Army veteran, opened by citing the VA's latest suicide-prevention report. Released Sept. 30, 2026, and based on data through 2024, it counts 6,488 veteran suicides in 2024, about 17.7 a day, as reported by The American Legion; her figures are in line with that. Ranking Member Julia Brownley (D-Calif.), who is not seeking re-election and noted this was the last hearing the two would lead together, asked how many veterans could be eligible. Andrew Gradison of the Veterans Health Administration said about nine million veterans are enrolled in VA health care and about 6.4 million actively use it; of those, roughly 2.1 million have been diagnosed with treatment-resistant depression, major depressive disorder or post-traumatic stress disorder. Applying study criteria, he said roughly 180,000 might be eligible. He told Rep. Maggie Goodlander (D-N.H.) it is his estimate, not a firm number, and his written testimony does not include it. At a conference in New York in October, as reported by Politico and quoted by Newsweek, Gradison said it would be a challenge "in the first year to treat four digits' worth of patients"; at the hearing, Goodlander asked about that remark, and no answer was picked up on the recording. Dr. Ilse Wiechers of the VA said no decision had been made on whether veterans would sign written consent before treatment; she said the VA would wait for the FDA's review and label, and noted that the VA already requires written consent for ketamine and esketamine.

  2. Exhibit B

    The road to approval

    On April 18, 2026, President Trump signed Executive Order 14401, "Accelerating Medical Treatments for Serious Mental Illness." It says psychedelic drugs "show potential in clinical studies," and it directs the health agencies to work with the VA. In July the VA and the Department of Health and Human Services signed a five-year agreement on research and data sharing, and the VA also signed an agreement with the FDA; the sources we found do not give its date. Rep. Jack Bergman (R-Mich.), a retired Marine general, asked what happens when the FDA approves a drug. Gradison described steps that include the federal drug schedules and the states. He said three new drug applications are pending with the FDA and did not name them. Neither MDMA nor psilocybin is approved by the FDA for any use, and federal rules list both in Schedule I. In August 2024 the FDA declined to approve an earlier application for MDMA-assisted therapy. A different drug, esketamine (Spravato), a nasal spray related to ketamine, is already approved for depression; it is not one of the classic psychedelics, but it came up several times as a model. The VA's August announcement of a psilocybin trial says it will consider clinical use outside research "only" after FDA approval; in written testimony, the VA said that as of September it was running 21 active clinical studies of psychedelic treatments. Members of both parties raised the same worry about clinics outside the VA: Rep. Greg Murphy (R-N.C.) said he shared concerns about ketamine clinics "popping up," and Brownley said the VA must be ready for a wave of private providers once a drug is approved. Asked by Miller-Meeks about veterans who travel to clinics in Mexico, Gradison said the VA does not endorse that; he said he had visited clinics there at the Secretary's direction, with FDA and HHS officials, and called what he found "encouraging," but the chairwoman moved to her last question before he finished.

  3. Exhibit C

    The ten sites

    The VA's written testimony says it has "identified ten facilities" to serve as "early implementers, educators, or sites-in-training." Dr. Wiechers said five of the ten would treat patients and train providers if a drug is approved, and one more would focus only on training. Training, she said, would include sitting in the room with someone who has done it before: "we don't want anyone flying blind." Delegate Kimberlyn King-Hinds (R-N. Mariana Islands) asked what this means for places far from any VA clinic; Gradison said it is "going to take time." Rep. Herb Conaway (D-N.J.), also a physician, said that looking at a list of locations he saw no sites in the southeast; those places came from a member, not from a VA announcement. Wiechers answered that the sites were those the VA evaluated as most likely to succeed early, and that it would learn from them before rolling out to more sites across the country. Rep. Abe Hamadeh (R-Ariz.) urged the VA to move faster and to include Arizona; Gradison said Arizona is "on our list" for next steps. Goodlander then asked where the sites are. Gradison said the VA has not made any public announcements about its initial ten sites. She asked for the list anyway, and a staffing plan with costs, within 30 days. The VA agreed to give the committee that information. That commitment is to the committee, not to the public. Thirty days from the hearing is Dec. 18, 2026.

  4. Exhibit D

    The people and the money

    Witnesses on both panels said these treatments take a lot of staff time; Gradison said a single dosing session can last six to eight hours. Conaway cited VA staffing-loss numbers that he attributed to the VA's own data; we could not find that data set, so they remain his figures. Wiechers did not address the figures directly; she said the VA is "actively recruiting and hiring" mental health professionals and is still meeting veterans' needs. Goodlander said the VA has about 500 fewer psychologists and psychiatrists than at the start of last year and asked for a plan; her figure matches a ProPublica analysis from March 2026. She asked whether the VA had asked for funding; Gradison said the fiscal year 2027 budget did not include a request for specific funding for this work. Miller-Meeks later cited the VA's overall mental health budget, up about $719 million from one year to the next; that figure is hers, it covers all mental health care rather than psychedelic therapy, and we could not check it against the VA's budget documents. On the second panel, Brownley asked all four outside experts, yes or no, whether the VA currently has sufficient clinical staff to implement psychedelic-assisted therapy; the answers differed. Dr. Leith States said about half of all veterans are not enrolled in the VA and that each VA medical center is at a different level of readiness. Brownley said the VA's interest in implementing these therapies quickly and safely was "totally at odds" with the current VA leadership's approach to hiring and keeping staff; that is her view. Dr. Raquel Halfond of the American Psychological Association summed up her priorities as "staffing, staffing, and staffing."

  5. Exhibit E

    Two warnings

    Rep. Morgan Luttrell (R-Texas), a former Navy SEAL, is not on the subcommittee; Chairwoman Miller-Meeks asked for consent for him to take part, and he said he has pushed for these treatments for years. He said that if the effort goes wrong, "this goes away forever," and that if you save one veteran, you have succeeded. The 2.6 million veterans he cited is his figure; the VA's witness gave 2.1 million across three diagnoses. The second warning came from Dr. Leith States, whom the chairwoman introduced as a former acting assistant secretary for health at HHS. He told the panel he is a combat veteran and gets all his care through the VA. He said that when the VA pays an outside clinic, "a referral cannot become a transfer of accountability." Dr. Marion Coe, who advises drug companies on these medicines, summarized responses to a 2024 request for information from a congressional caucus: the drugs "should not be considered interchangeable." MDMA, psilocybin and other compounds, the report found, carry different risks, and each should be judged on its own evidence.

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.

Open questions

  1. Will the VA give the committee its list of ten sites and its staffing plan, and will either be made public? As of Nov. 28, 2026, neither is public.
  2. Will the FDA approve a psychedelic therapy? As of Nov. 28, 2026, no classic psychedelic, and neither MDMA nor psilocybin, is approved.
  3. Who would be treated first? Asked, Gradison said the VA would follow principles of medical ethics; he did not describe a priority rule.
  4. Will the VA's budget include funding for this work? The fiscal year 2027 budget did not include a request for specific funding.
  5. If a treatment is approved and, as a VA official reportedly said in October, even a four-digit number of patients would be a challenge in the first year, who should decide who goes first?
