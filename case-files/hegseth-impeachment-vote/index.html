Case File #004

The Hegseth Impeachment Vote Came Down to 7. Here's the Roll Call

On Nov. 10, 2026, the House voted 211 to 204 to send Rep. Thomas Massie's eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee, which effectively ended the effort for this Congress. Eleven Democrats and seven Republicans did not vote, and the margin was seven. If only the eleven Democrats had been present and voted no, the motion would have failed, 211 to 215. If all eighteen had been present and voted with their parties, it would have passed, 218 to 215. The record shows that the missing Democrats decided this vote only if the missing Republicans stayed missing; whether they would have come back for a close vote, the record cannot tell us.

The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    The Charges

    On Nov. 9, Rep. Thomas Massie, a Kentucky Republican, gave formal notice on the House floor and read all eight articles, which took him more than an hour. Articles one to three say the secretary waged war on Iran in violation of the War Powers Resolution, the 1973 law that limits how long American forces can fight without approval from Congress. Article four says he dismantled the Pentagon's programs for preventing civilian harm, and points to a strike on an elementary school in southern Iran on Feb. 28, the first morning of the strikes on Iran. Article five concerns the military strikes on boats that the administration says were smuggling drugs in the Caribbean and the eastern Pacific; the resolution puts the number killed at 243 at least and calls the strikes extrajudicial killings. Article six says the secretary used the Department of Defense to retaliate against Sen. Mark Kelly of Arizona, a retired Navy captain, for a November 2025 video addressed to service members. Article seven concerns the operation that captured Venezuela's president, Nicolás Maduro, which the resolution calls an act of war carried out without a declaration from Congress. Article eight concerns the 2025 campaign against the Houthis in Yemen and quotes the secretary's own words from the group chat about that operation. All of these are charges. None of them has been tested in a trial.

  2. Exhibit B

    The Defense

    On the morning of Nov. 10, hours before the vote, Secretary Hegseth answered at the Pentagon. He called the effort the work of "one disgruntled and defeated congressman"; he did not name Rep. Massie, who lost his primary in May and leaves Congress in January. The secretary said that in 250 years, "no secretary of war or secretary of defense has ever been impeached for carrying out lawful military operations." A secretary of war has been impeached: William Belknap, in 1876, on corruption charges, and the Senate acquitted him; none has been impeached over military operations. The secretary said each operation was lawful, ordered by the president and reported to Congress, and that the disagreement is about policy, not crimes. On civilian deaths, he said the claim that the American military targets civilians "is a lie" and "a slander against every professional who flew, sailed and fought on these missions." He did not mention the school. Whether these operations were lawful is exactly what the two sides dispute.

  3. Exhibit C

    The Vote

    That afternoon, Rep. Massie called up his resolution, H.Res. 1655, and the clerk read it in full into the record. Rep. Darrell Issa, a California Republican, moved to refer it to the Judiciary Committee. He had an hour to argue for the motion and used none of it. The chair called a voice vote and ruled that the ayes had it; Rep. Massie asked for a recorded vote. The vote was scheduled for fifteen minutes and stayed open for about twenty-seven. The result, from the Clerk of the House, Roll Call 317: 211 yes, 204 no. Republicans voted 210 yes and one no, Rep. Massie himself. Democrats voted zero yes and 203 no. One more yes came from Rep. Kevin Kiley of California, listed by the Clerk as an independent; he left the Republican Party in March and still sits with the Republican conference. Eighteen members did not vote.

  4. Exhibit D

    The Missing Eighteen

    Not voting, Democrats (11): Julia Brownley, Lou Correa and Nancy Pelosi of California; Laura Gillen of New York; Steven Horsford of Nevada; Jonathan Jackson of Illinois; Brittany Pettersen of Colorado; Melanie Stansbury of New Mexico; Haley Stevens of Michigan; Marilyn Strickland of Washington; Frederica Wilson of Florida. Not voting, Republicans (7): Monica De La Cruz of Texas; Neal Dunn of Florida; Russell Fry of South Carolina; Julia Letlow of Louisiana; Tom Tiffany and Derrick Van Orden of Wisconsin; Rudy Yakym of Indiana. First way: if the eleven Democrats had been present and voted no, and nothing else changed, the motion fails 211 to 215. Second way: if all eighteen had been present and each voted with their party, it passes 218 to 215. If the motion had failed, Secretary Hegseth would not have been impeached; the articles would have stayed on the floor for another procedural motion or a direct vote. According to ABC News, several members said a police escort and traffic near the Capitol held them up. Reps. Pettersen and Brownley said, in statements reported by The Washington Post, that Republicans closed the vote before they could cast theirs. Rep. Jackson told TMZ, as quoted by Newsweek, "I should have been able to make the vote; it was a procedural vote." Rep. Correa had announced a medical absence in advance. Rep. Horsford cited a personal emergency and said on the House floor that he took full responsibility. Speaker Emerita Pelosi's spokesperson said she was in San Francisco for a Veterans Day event and would have voted no on the motion to refer. The Washington Post reported that Rep. Stansbury posted a video apology. This file did not find public explanations from the seven absent Republicans.

  5. Exhibit E

    What Comes Next

    The day before the vote, Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries spoke about the resolution at a press conference and predicted Republican support for it. On the referral vote, one Republican voted with the Democrats, and he was the one who wrote the articles. Democrats won the House on Nov. 3. When the new Congress is sworn in on Jan. 3, these articles expire with the old one. A new resolution would have to be filed, and in a House with a Democratic majority, a referral motion would need Democratic votes to pass.

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.

  5. Nov. 10, 2026Morning: Secretary Hegseth answers at the Pentagon. Afternoon: Rep. Massie calls up the resolution and the clerk reads it. Evening (6:02 p.m. Eastern, per the Clerk): the House votes 211 to 204 to refer the articles to the Judiciary Committee (Roll Call 317).

Open questions

  1. The headline was eleven missing Democrats. The roll call also shows seven missing Republicans. Which number tells you more about the vote?
  2. Any single member can force an impeachment vote. Will the new majority use it, and will the new minority answer with the same motion to refer?
  3. The secretary says these were policy disagreements. The articles say they were crimes. Who should decide that: the House, the Senate, or the courts?
