Case File #009

CISA Nominee on Jan. 6: "I Don't Believe It Was an Attack"

On Thursday, Nov. 19, 2026, the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee, chaired by Sen. Rand Paul (R-Ky.), with Sen. Gary Peters (D-Mich.) as ranking member, considered thirteen nominations in two panels. This file focuses on two. Michael LoSapio, chief information security officer at Palantir Technologies, is nominated to lead CISA, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, which has had no Senate-confirmed director since January 2025. Eric Ueland, deputy director for management at the White House budget office, is nominated to be Comptroller General, head of the Government Accountability Office, for a fifteen-year term. Asked whether there was an attack on the Capitol on Jan. 6, 2021, Mr. LoSapio first said "I don't know," then "I don't believe it was an attack." Mr. Ueland said: "I'm not here as a plant, a stooge, a lackey for anybody." Democrats criticized the process that produced the GAO nomination; Chairman Paul said the process is no different than before. As of this writing, the committee has not posted a date for a vote.

The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    Thirteen Nominees, Two Panels

    The Senate's recording runs about seventy-seven minutes, first words to adjournment. Besides Mr. LoSapio and Mr. Ueland, the nominees included the head of the Office of Government Ethics, four inspectors general and four judges for the District of Columbia's Superior Court, the city's trial court. CISA helps agencies, states and companies defend their networks, and it supports election security. The GAO audits federal agencies for Congress; the Comptroller General's term is fifteen years. Sen. Maggie Hassan (D-N.H.) asked all seven nominees on the first panel whether, if directed by the President to take an action that would break the law, they would follow the law or the President's directive. Each answered: "Follow the law."

  2. Exhibit B

    The Cyber Agency's Budget

    Senators from both parties brought up the week of outages earlier this month that disrupted flights, email and banking. Sen. Elissa Slotkin (D-Mich.) said the administration "cut about a thousand positions from CISA in the past year and a half," and cited a $385 million cut. That figure is close to the net cut proposed for CISA in the President's request for fiscal year 2027, as reported by trade press; other counts of the same request run from about $360 million, net, to $707 million before transfers. The thousand, per Federal News Network, is employees CISA has lost since January 2025; the new request would cut about 867 positions, according to trade press. It is a request; Congress has not enacted it. Mr. LoSapio said he would "get involved and understand where the gaps are," and that he does not "necessarily believe more money means more security"; "spending money on the right solutions in the right ways actually decreases risk." Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-Ohio) asked about the next funding deadline; CyberScoop noted that Homeland Security components keep operating during a funding lapse, and that past shutdowns have slowed agency work.

  3. Exhibit C

    Two Questions About the Past

    Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.) asked several nominees who won the popular vote in 2020 and whether the Capitol was attacked on Jan. 6, 2021. Mr. LoSapio declined to answer the 2020 question; "disqualified" was Sen. Blumenthal's judgment, and it followed that answer. On Jan. 6, the order as recorded: "I don't know." Then, "I don't believe it was an attack." Then, "I wasn't involved in politics. I know what I saw on the news." Asked again: "No." Other nominees gave their own answers: inspector general nominees Mark Priebe and Dennis Kirk said Joe Biden won the popular vote; Michael Chamberlain, for Government Ethics, said "Joe Biden was the president for four years"; on the second panel, Robert Steinau said of Jan. 6, "I would call that an attack," and Maria D'Avanzo described "a chaotic protest" in which "some people violated the law." Chairman Paul objected. He said judges shouldn't be answering political questions, called the riot "terrible" and "gross," argued some who entered the Capitol were punished out of proportion, and said: "I object to the series of questions."

  4. Exhibit D

    The Law That Picks a Comptroller General

    When the office is vacant, a commission of ten congressional leaders forms: the top leaders of both chambers, plus the chairs and ranking members of this committee and House Oversight. It "shall recommend at least 3 individuals," and the President may ask for more. The text does not say, in those words, that the President must choose from the list; the GAO's own description says that, typically, the President then selects from those recommended. Sen. James Lankford (R-Okla.), who supports Mr. Ueland, said the White House seemed to follow the letter of the law and that he is not completely sure it followed the entire spirit. Ranking Member Peters said "there has been no process at all," and that the committee had to "rush through these thirteen nominees at once." A Democratic committee aide told Government Executive that Democrats got about a day to submit names. Chairman Paul said the position had been open for nearly a year, Democrats submitted no names, and the process is no different than it has been. Sen. Hassan described the last selection as separate lists sent after "a thorough bipartisan process for a year," with Gene Dodaro, the last Comptroller General, on both.

  5. Exhibit E

    The GAO Nominee, in His Own Words

    Under the Impoundment Control Act, when the executive branch withholds money Congress approved, the Comptroller General can go to court. Ranking Member Peters said the GAO has issued twelve reports finding agencies broke the law under this administration; that is his count. Mr. Ueland said he had not reviewed the reports Sen. Peters's staff sent him and took "an affirmative decision not to," so as not to prejudge the GAO's work. When Sen. Peters summed up his position as "checks and balances be damned," Mr. Ueland replied: "Senator, that's an incorrect takeaway of what I just said." Asked by Chairman Paul whether he would make all decisions in a nonpartisan way, he said: "I'm not here as a plant, a stooge, a lackey for anybody." Asked whether he would ignore a White House request to stop an investigation: "To be clear. Yes." His current boss, OMB Director Russell Vought, called the GAO in September 2025, as reported by Axios, "something that shouldn't exist." Mr. Ueland told the committee in writing that Mr. Vought asked him once, in passing, if he wanted the job.

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.

The timeline

  1. Jan. 2025From this month on, CISA has no Senate-confirmed director.
  2. Sept. 2025OMB Director Russell Vought calls the GAO "something that shouldn't exist," as reported by Axios.
  3. Nov. 19, 2026Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee hearing on thirteen nominations, including CISA and the GAO.
  4. Nov. 20, 2026Hearing record closes at 5 p.m. for written statements and questions.

Open questions

  1. When will the committee vote? It has not posted a date; The Hill, citing Politico, reports Chairman Paul said a vote on Mr. LoSapio will be held in early December. No date has been found for Mr. Ueland.
  2. Will the announced holds be placed? Sen. Ron Wyden told Nextgov he will place one on Mr. LoSapio's nomination; Sen. Thom Tillis told reporters he plans one, Federal News Network reports. No formal notice has been seen in the Senate record.
  3. Will the nominations be confirmed before the session ends? Under Senate rules, nominations not confirmed when a session ends can be returned to the President.
  4. Will the written questions and answers be published, and will the White House or either nominee respond?
