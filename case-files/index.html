  1. Case File #013

    Kennedy on Drug Prices: "It's the Intermediaries" and Six Bills

    On Tuesday, Nov. 17, 2026, the Senate Judiciary Committee held a hearing titled "Prescribing Sunshine: How Competition & Transparency Lowers Prescription Drug Costs." Sen. John Kennedy (R-La.) said of the market for prescription drugs: "It's the intermediaries." The intermediaries are pharmacy benefit managers, or PBMs, which set prices, decide which drugs a plan covers, process claims and build pharmacy networks. In its July 2024 interim staff report, the Federal Trade Commission's staff found that the three largest PBMs handled nearly 80 percent of prescriptions in 2023 and the six largest about 94 percent; the study is not finished. Five witnesses testified: a patient advocate, the co-founder of an online pharmacy that competes with the pharmacies the big PBMs own, the head of the Iowa Pharmacy Association, a health economist and the chief executive of a Chicago health center. No PBM, insurer or drug manufacturer testified. The committee chairman, Sen. Chuck Grassley (R-Iowa), kept returning to six bills the committee approved by voice vote on April 3, 2025. Five deal with patents and generic competition; one would require the FTC to finish its PBM study. One, the Affordable Prescriptions for Patients Act (S. 1041), passed the Senate by unanimous consent on July 21, 2026; it has not become law, and the House has not acted. The other five have been on the Senate calendar since April 10, 2025, and congress.gov shows no floor action. Kennedy said "I am embarrassed that my side has been in control for two years, and we've done nothing"; the chairman answered that the committee has marked up six bills. This file does not rule on who is to blame: it sets out what was said, what the documents say, and what is still open.

  2. Case File #012

    "A Legal Ponzi Scheme": Johnson, Social Security and the 2032 Date

    On Wednesday, Nov. 18, 2026, Sen. Ron Johnson (R-Wis.), chairman of the Senate Budget Committee, opened a hearing by saying: "The title of this hearing is Social Security the Reality, a Legal Ponzi Scheme." Seventy million people received Social Security benefits in December 2025. That morning he had given a television interview; during the hearing, three Democratic senators (Jeff Merkley of Oregon, Mark Warner of Virginia and Ben Ray Luján of New Mexico) read aloud what they said he told the interviewer, and he answered each time. He called his remark "imprecise" and said he is not saying to shut down Social Security; his proposal, he said, is a law so that benefits are not reduced. The Social Security Trustees' 2026 report projects that the retirement (OASI) trust fund's reserves run out in the last quarter of 2032, when incoming money would pay 78 percent of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts; counted together with the disability fund, the date is 2034, at 83 percent. On the day of the hearing the chairman introduced S. 5765, which was sent to the Finance Committee. This file does not rule on the label: it sets out what was said, how the chairman answered, what the Trustees' report says, and what is still open.

  3. Case File #011

    CFTC Nominees Asked: Is a Contract on an NFL Game a Sports Bet?

    On Tuesday, Nov. 17, 2026, the Senate Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry Committee, chaired by Sen. John Boozman (R-Ark.) with Sen. Amy Klobuchar (D-Minn.) as ranking member, heard two nominees to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission: Nathan Anonick, nominated for a Republican seat, and Matthew Rowland, nominated for a Democratic seat. The CFTC has had one member, Chairman Michael Selig, since December 2025. The question running through the hearing: is a prediction-market contract on who wins a football game a sports bet, which states and tribes regulate, or a federally regulated contract? Federal courts have not agreed. Asked by Sen. Adam Schiff (D-Calif.) about the Colts–Texans game, Mr. Anonick said federal courts have disagreed on that. Two days later, on Nov. 19, the Senate passed S. 4035, the DEATH BETS Act, on contracts tied to war, terrorism, assassination, U.S. military operations and a person's death; the House has not referred it to a committee. By its own text, the bill leaves the sports question where it was.

  4. Case File #010

    "Did Oracle Keep the Promise?" Oracle CEO Sicilia on the VA Contract

    On Wednesday, Nov. 18, 2026, the Senate Veterans' Affairs Committee, chaired by Sen. Jerry Moran (R-Kan.), with Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.) as ranking member, heard from Dr. Neil Evans, who runs the VA's health record program; Mike Sicilia, one of Oracle's two chief executives, with Oracle executive Seema Verma; and Carol Harris of the GAO. On Thursday, Nov. 19, the House Veterans' Affairs Committee, chaired by Rep. Mike Bost (R-Ill.), with Rep. Mark Takano (D-Calif.) as ranking member, heard from Mr. Sicilia, appearing under a congressional subpoena, and Ms. Verma. The subject: the electronic health record the VA contracted for in 2018 with Cerner, which Oracle bought in 2022. In August 2026, the VA raised the contract's ceiling, the most the VA may order, from just under $10 billion to about $26.94 billion. A ceiling is not a bill: the work is bought through task orders, each at a fixed price. Asked by Rep. Maggie Goodlander (D-N.H.) whether Oracle kept the promise it made to the committee in 2022, Mr. Sicilia said he has "explained many times why I believe yes," adding that he "can't do the question justice with yes or no." He said the scope of the work had changed. As of Nov. 21, 2026, a House subpoena calls for Oracle's chairman, Larry Ellison, to appear on Dec. 10, according to the committee's ranking member; the committee's public calendar lists no hearing for that date.

  5. Case File #009

    CISA Nominee on Jan. 6: "I Don't Believe It Was an Attack"

    On Thursday, Nov. 19, 2026, the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee, chaired by Sen. Rand Paul (R-Ky.), with Sen. Gary Peters (D-Mich.) as ranking member, considered thirteen nominations in two panels. This file focuses on two. Michael LoSapio, chief information security officer at Palantir Technologies, is nominated to lead CISA, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, which has had no Senate-confirmed director since January 2025. Eric Ueland, deputy director for management at the White House budget office, is nominated to be Comptroller General, head of the Government Accountability Office, for a fifteen-year term. Asked whether there was an attack on the Capitol on Jan. 6, 2021, Mr. LoSapio first said "I don't know," then "I don't believe it was an attack." Mr. Ueland said: "I'm not here as a plant, a stooge, a lackey for anybody." Democrats criticized the process that produced the GAO nomination; Chairman Paul said the process is no different than before. As of this writing, the committee has not posted a date for a vote.

  6. Case File #008

    Roblox's CEO Was Invited to Testify. He Did Not Appear.

    On Nov. 17, 2026, the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime and Counterterrorism held a hearing titled "Roblox and Online Gaming: Digital Playgrounds Endangering America's Children." Roblox's chief executive, David Baszucki, was invited and did not appear; Sen. Josh Hawley said Mr. Baszucki sent him a letter that morning, which has not been made public. Much of the hearing came down to one question: what happens when a family tries to take Roblox to court. Law professor Myriam Gilles testified that roughly 180 families have sued and that Roblox's answer has been its arbitration clause; CBS News counted nearly 200 lawsuits in federal court. These are allegations; no court has ruled on whether they are true. On Nov. 16, the day before the hearing, a Roblox spokesperson told CBS News that the claims being made about Roblox are "fundamentally misleading." Two bills to limit forced arbitration for children were introduced the day before; neither has had a committee vote. This hearing was about the sexual exploitation of children; this file does not repeat the details of any case.

  7. Case File #007

    A Week of Outages: What Officials Told Congress Under Oath

    From Nov. 12 to Nov. 17, 2026, a series of outages and disruptions affected Microsoft 365, JPMorgan Chase, United Airlines, Apple and an internal NSA network, and the FAA held every departing flight in the country. Most organizations that explained their outages gave a technical cause of their own: a software defect, a software bug in hardware systems, an internal update, routine maintenance; for the ground stop, the Transportation Secretary described overloaded routers and old hardware. None, in its own statement as reported, described an attack. On Nov. 18, at the House Homeland Security Committee's annual worldwide threats hearing, Rep. Michael Guest (R-Miss.) asked whether the outages were related. Under oath, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said, "We have seen nothing that would indicate that they are related"; Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton said, "we have no indication that these incidents are related"; and FBI Deputy Director Christopher Raia said, "At this point, there is no indication whatsoever that there is any cyber malicious activity related to any of those incidents." "No indication" is what investigators have found so far; none of them called it a final report. Still open: the FAA's investigation of the ground stop, Microsoft's final report, the full cause of the NSA outage, and United's cause.

  8. Case File #006

    The USS Lincoln Record: Hung Cao at His Navy Secretary Hearing

    On Tuesday, Nov. 17, 2026, acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao appeared before the Senate Armed Services Committee for his confirmation hearing. The sharpest exchanges were about the aircraft carrier USS Abraham Lincoln and its strike group, and how their long deployment had been described: in an Aug. 14 statement on the Navy's website ("a small number of mental health cases were treated with no loss of life"), in a September letter to Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand, as reported by The Maritime Executive ("a total of eight suicide attempts across the strike group", no deaths), and at the hearing ("these are eight mitigations we're able to mitigate"). The August and September descriptions are not the same measure: one spoke of the carrier, the other counted the whole strike group over a longer period. Senators also asked about conditions, costs and what Congress is told. Because he left active duty in October 2021, federal law bars his appointment before October 2028 unless Congress passes a waiver. As of Nov. 18, we found no announced date for a committee vote or a waiver. If you or someone you know is struggling: in the U.S., call or text 988; veterans, service members and their families can dial 988, then press 1, or text 838255 (Veterans Crisis Line).

  9. Case File #005

    What Stopped U.S. Flights? Four Explanations in Four Days

    On Friday, Nov. 13, 2026, at about 3:30 p.m. Eastern, the FAA's air traffic network became unstable, and the agency stopped departures across the United States for more than four hours. About 3,500 aircraft in the air landed safely. Over the next four days the cause was described four ways: old equipment overloaded, with the new equipment holding up (Secretary Duffy, Friday); new equipment overwhelming the older system (Secretary Mullin, Friday); by the FAA's initial investigation, a new router at a remote site flooding an old network (Secretary Duffy on Saturday, the FAA in writing on Sunday); and old equipment plus copper-to-fiber work under the previous administration (President Trump, Monday). The first three are not the same explanation, but they can partly fit together; the record does not yet show which work the president is referring to. The FAA's review of the root cause is still open.

  10. Case File #004

    The Hegseth Impeachment Vote Came Down to 7. Here's the Roll Call

    On Nov. 10, 2026, the House voted 211 to 204 to send Rep. Thomas Massie's eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee, which effectively ended the effort for this Congress. Eleven Democrats and seven Republicans did not vote, and the margin was seven. If only the eleven Democrats had been present and voted no, the motion would have failed, 211 to 215. If all eighteen had been present and voted with their parties, it would have passed, 218 to 215. The record shows that the missing Democrats decided this vote only if the missing Republicans stayed missing; whether they would have come back for a close vote, the record cannot tell us.

  11. Case File #003

    Racing to Confirm Trump's Judges: Did Democrats Do the Same in 2024?

    Democrats won control of the Senate on Nov. 3, 2026. Until the new Senate takes office on Jan. 3, 2027, Republicans hold the majority, and they are moving to confirm as many of President Trump's judicial nominees as they can. At a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing on Nov. 10, Sen. Dick Durbin said the chairman is setting aside the committee's long-standing practices. Chairman Chuck Grassley answered that Democrats did the same in the 2024 lame duck. The confirmation counts match the chairman's number, and both parties have used a lame duck to confirm judges after losing an election; whether past chairmen also shortened the committee's timelines is not settled by the public record this file has found.

At prosecutors' request, court orders barred the phone companies from telling them. At a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing on September 29, 2026, senators and Mr. Smith disagreed about why the secrecy was needed and whether it was lawful.
