Case File #008

Roblox's CEO Was Invited to Testify. He Did Not Appear.

On Nov. 17, 2026, the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime and Counterterrorism held a hearing titled "Roblox and Online Gaming: Digital Playgrounds Endangering America's Children." Roblox's chief executive, David Baszucki, was invited and did not appear; Sen. Josh Hawley said Mr. Baszucki sent him a letter that morning, which has not been made public. Much of the hearing came down to one question: what happens when a family tries to take Roblox to court. Law professor Myriam Gilles testified that roughly 180 families have sued and that Roblox's answer has been its arbitration clause; CBS News counted nearly 200 lawsuits in federal court. These are allegations; no court has ruled on whether they are true. On Nov. 16, the day before the hearing, a Roblox spokesperson told CBS News that the claims being made about Roblox are "fundamentally misleading." Two bills to limit forced arbitration for children were introduced the day before; neither has had a committee vote. This hearing was about the sexual exploitation of children; this file does not repeat the details of any case.

The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    The Invitation

    On Aug. 12, 2026, Sen. Josh Hawley (R-Mo.) and Sen. Dick Durbin (D-Ill.), the subcommittee's top Democrat, opened a joint inquiry into Roblox and asked for documents by Aug. 31. At the Nov. 17 hearing, four witnesses were sworn in: two who described being targeted on the platform as children, Khari James of the National Center on Sexual Exploitation, and Northwestern law professor Myriam Gilles. Roblox's chief executive, David Baszucki, was invited and did not appear. Sen. Hawley said a letter from Mr. Baszucki arrived that morning; its text has not been made public. In his opening statement Sen. Hawley said signing up takes only a birthday and a username, and acknowledged that Roblox has added steps before users can send chat messages.

  2. Exhibit B

    The Contract

    Every Roblox user agrees to terms of use that include an arbitration clause. Prof. Gilles testified that roughly 180 families have sued Roblox, consolidated before Judge Seeborg in the Northern District of California, and that Roblox's answer to each case has been that the user agreed to arbitrate. CBS News, on Nov. 16, counted nearly 200 lawsuits in federal court; the two numbers may not count the same thing. Sen. Durbin asked whether a child can consent to arbitration; Prof. Gilles described two arguments made by Roblox and Discord, as she reads their filings: that an arbitrator, not a judge, decides whether a child can cancel the agreement, and that a child who keeps playing has accepted it. She said at least two courts have accepted the second argument, and that co-defendants Snap and Meta decided not to pursue arbitration.

  3. Exhibit C

    One Thousand Dollars

    Sen. Hawley described a clause in the same terms of use that, in his words, limits damages for all claims against Roblox to $1,000 in total. Deep Account has not independently confirmed the dollar figure in the current terms. Prof. Gilles said that amount would not change a company's behavior, and that in her view the cap and the one-year deadline to bring a claim would not be enforceable in court, which, she argues, is why it matters where these cases are heard.

  4. Exhibit D

    The Numbers

    Sen. Durbin cited a company figure: in 2025, Roblox submitted 65,381 reports of suspected child exploitation to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children. A report is not a confirmed crime and not a count of victims; one case can produce more than one report. According to press reports, Roblox has told Sen. Chuck Grassley's office that its detection systems keep improving and that better detection brings more reports to the surface. Senators and witnesses read the number as a measure of how much abuse the platform attracts.

  5. Exhibit E

    What Roblox Says

    August 2026, Matt Kaufman, Roblox chief safety officer, to Kotaku: "Roblox is built on the foundation of safety. We do not compromise on that commitment." Nov. 16, 2026, a Roblox spokesperson to CBS News: "The claims being made about Roblox are fundamentally misleading and ignore the rigorous safeguards we have in place to help protect users," listing mandatory age checks before users can chat, content limited by age, a ban on sharing photos and videos in chat, and continuous monitoring and filtering of chat. That statement came before the hearing, not in response to it. As of publication, we have not found a statement from Roblox issued after the hearing; if the company issues one, this file will add it. Prof. Gilles said she believes the timing of recent safety changes reflects state lawsuits and the subcommittee's investigation; Roblox has not described its changes that way. Prof. Gilles also said arbitration has a place when a family chooses it after a dispute arises.

  6. Exhibit F

    The Bills

    On Mon., Nov. 16, two bills were introduced: the No Forced Arbitration for Child Sexual Exploitation and Cyber Injuries Act (Sens. Hawley and Durbin), which according to CBS News would let families go to court, including as a group, even if their child clicked an arbitration agreement; and the Ending Forced Arbitration of Children Online Act (Sens. Katie Britt, Richard Blumenthal and Cory Booker). Neither has had a committee vote. Sen. Blumenthal argued that tech companies support regulation in public and block it in private, citing the Kids Online Safety Act, which the Senate passed 91-3 in July 2024 as part of a larger package and the House did not vote on. Sen. Durbin, who leaves the Senate in January, wants his separate Stop CSAM Act passed with the annual defense bill.

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.
