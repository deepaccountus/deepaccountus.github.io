Case File #011

CFTC Nominees Asked: Is a Contract on an NFL Game a Sports Bet?

On Tuesday, Nov. 17, 2026, the Senate Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry Committee, chaired by Sen. John Boozman (R-Ark.) with Sen. Amy Klobuchar (D-Minn.) as ranking member, heard two nominees to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission: Nathan Anonick, nominated for a Republican seat, and Matthew Rowland, nominated for a Democratic seat. The CFTC has had one member, Chairman Michael Selig, since December 2025. The question running through the hearing: is a prediction-market contract on who wins a football game a sports bet, which states and tribes regulate, or a federally regulated contract? Federal courts have not agreed. Asked by Sen. Adam Schiff (D-Calif.) about the Colts–Texans game, Mr. Anonick said federal courts have disagreed on that. Two days later, on Nov. 19, the Senate passed S. 4035, the DEATH BETS Act, on contracts tied to war, terrorism, assassination, U.S. military operations and a person's death; the House has not referred it to a committee. By its own text, the bill leaves the sports question where it was.

The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    A Commission of One

    By law the CFTC has five commissioners, no more than three from one party. For nearly a year it has had one: Chairman Michael Selig, confirmed in December 2025. The committee's chairman, Sen. John Boozman (R-Ark.), and its ranking member, Sen. Amy Klobuchar (D-Minn.), opened the Nov. 17, 2026 hearing. If both nominees are confirmed, the commission would have three members, not five. Nathan Anonick, nominated for a Republican seat, has advised Chairman Boozman for almost six years and works on the committee's staff. Matthew Rowland, nominated for a Democratic seat, has worked at the CFTC since 2015 and is on loan to the committee's Democratic staff. Asked by Sen. Raphael Warnock (D-Ga.) whether a full commission, with at least two members of each party, is essential, both said yes.

  2. Exhibit B

    The Legal Map

    In Murphy v. NCAA (2018) the Supreme Court struck down a federal law that barred states from authorizing sports betting; the Court also wrote: "Congress can regulate sports gambling directly, but if it elects not to do so, each State is free to act on its own." The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act governs gaming on tribal lands. The Commodity Exchange Act lists contracts the CFTC may find contrary to the public interest, among them gaming, war, terrorism and assassination. In Sen. Klobuchar's words, the CFTC "has taken the position that states are preempted from regulating sporting event contracts offered on prediction markets"; according to press reports of its lawsuits, the agency says these contracts are swaps under its exclusive jurisdiction. The courts are split at an early stage: the Third Circuit upheld, two to one, an order blocking New Jersey from applying its gambling laws to the exchange Kalshi (April 2026); the Ninth Circuit ruled for Nevada (August); the Sixth Circuit ruled for Ohio and Tennessee (September). None is a final ruling on the merits. Mr. Rowland told Sen. Klobuchar the commission has a rulemaking under way, that a Supreme Court decision is likely within the next term or the one after, and that the agency would be bound by it.

  3. Exhibit C

    "Quacks Like a Bet"

    Every senator who raised the sports question at this hearing was a Democrat; the two Republicans who spoke, Chairman Boozman and Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith (R-Miss.), asked about other things. Sen. Tina Smith (D-Minn.), whose state enacted a law in May banning prediction market platforms, sports contracts included (the CFTC went to court to block it), asked the nominees about the Murphy decision, tribes and the agency's own rules, then whether there is any practical difference between a sports wager and a sport event contract. She said: "it is unambiguous that a prohibition on gaming applies to these event contracts." That is her legal view. Mr. Rowland said he knows "there have been courts that have held both ways on this issue"; Mr. Anonick said federal courts have disagreed and he would not want to tip the scales. Neither answered yes or no.

  4. Exhibit D

    A Blank Slate

    Sen. Adam Schiff (D-Calif.) said Chairman Selig, at his Nov. 19, 2025 confirmation hearing, said he would come to the question of whether prediction markets are gambling "with a blank slate and look to the courts" (S. Hrg. 119-214). Sen. Schiff's view of what followed: "he has been anything but a blank slate." That is the senator's view. In court, the agency argues that federal law gives it exclusive jurisdiction over these contracts and preempts state gambling laws; the Third Circuit has so far sided with that reading, the Ninth and the Sixth have not. As of Nov. 22, 2026, we have found no statement from the CFTC or Chairman Selig responding to this hearing. Sen. Schiff then put the Colts–Texans question to Mr. Anonick, then asked whether he is "a blank slate"; Mr. Anonick said he can only speak for himself, that the question is legally complex, and, asked whether he would take the chairman's position in court: "I'm not trying to be evasive." A decision, he said, would mean talking to the agency's lawyers, the tribes and the states.

  5. Exhibit E

    The Rest of the Job

    Staffing came up in both parties. Mr. Rowland, himself a CFTC employee, spoke of "significant staff attrition and disruption that took place in 2025." Sen. Michael Bennet (D-Colo.) gave figures: since 2024, contracts listed for trading up 167 percent and staff down about 25 percent (his figures; we could not verify them). Asked by Sen. Ben Ray Luján (D-N.M.), Mr. Anonick said: "There's been staffing reductions." Chairman Boozman spoke about resources, and Sen. Hyde-Smith asked about 24-7 trading expanding into agricultural futures markets and the producers who use them to hedge risk; Mr. Anonick said he would work with farm groups and the agency's agricultural advisory committee.

  6. Exhibit F

    The Bill That Passed

    On Thursday evening, Nov. 19, 2026, the Senate took up S. 4035, the DEATH BETS Act, introduced by Sen. Schiff in March; Sen. John Curtis (R-Utah) became a cosponsor on Sept. 30 and the two offered the amendment adopted that evening. There was no objection to taking up the bill or to the amendment; it passed by voice vote (no video; this is from the Congressional Record). As passed, exchanges and clearinghouses registered with the CFTC may not list or clear contracts that expressly reference terrorism, assassination, war, U.S. military operations or similar activity the commission defines by rule, nor contracts that, when listed, are reasonably expected to resolve principally based on a death of an individual. A final section says the bill does not affect whether other contracts on the Commodity Exchange Act's list, gaming among them, are permitted. Sen. Schiff said, according to the Record, that nothing in the act should have any effect on the pending lawsuits over sports contracts; Sen. Curtis said "Calling something a financial contract does not change the fact that it is gambling." The two senators have a separate bill, the Prediction Markets Are Gambling Act, on sports and casino-style contracts; it has not passed. On Friday the House received S. 4035 and held it at the desk; it has not been referred to a committee. An earlier House version, H.R. 7942 (Rep. Mike Levin, D-Calif.), has cosponsors from both parties and sits in a House Agriculture subcommittee.

Open questions

  1. Will the committee vote on the nominees? As of Nov. 22, 2026, the committee has not scheduled a vote; the Senate holds brief pro forma sessions until Nov. 30.
  2. Will the House act on S. 4035? As of Nov. 22, 2026, the House has received the bill and held it at the desk; it has not been referred to a committee.
  3. Who sets the rules for a contract on a football game? The sports question remains with the courts and, if it chooses, with Congress; none of the appeals rulings so far is final on the merits.
  4. Will the CFTC or Chairman Selig respond to the hearing? As of Nov. 22, 2026, we have found no statement responding to it.
  5. What happens in January? A new Congress convenes; bills not passed by then must start over, and pending nominations are generally returned to the President.
