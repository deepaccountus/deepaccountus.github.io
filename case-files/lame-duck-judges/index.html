Case File #003

Racing to Confirm Trump's Judges: Did Democrats Do the Same in 2024?

Democrats won control of the Senate on Nov. 3, 2026. Until the new Senate takes office on Jan. 3, 2027, Republicans hold the majority, and they are moving to confirm as many of President Trump's judicial nominees as they can. At a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing on Nov. 10, Sen. Dick Durbin said the chairman is setting aside the committee's long-standing practices. Chairman Chuck Grassley answered that Democrats did the same in the 2024 lame duck. The confirmation counts match the chairman's number, and both parties have used a lame duck to confirm judges after losing an election; whether past chairmen also shortened the committee's timelines is not settled by the public record this file has found.

Subject
Republicans' push to confirm judges before Democrats take control of the Senate, and the dispute over the committee's practices

A through E
Status

The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    The Charge

    Sen. Dick Durbin, the committee's ranking Democrat, listed four practices he says the chairman is setting aside: hearings no closer than two weeks apart, 28 days to review a nominee's questionnaire before the hearing, a cleared FBI background check before a nominee is called, and fewer nominees at a time. He says both parties have followed these, and that the chairman is setting them aside without the minority's consent. He pointed to two Texas nominees who, he said, are slated for the committee's next hearing; one of them, Elliot Gaiser, has led the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel in this administration, and Sen. Durbin says his questionnaire reached the committee ten days before that hearing. (The committee's page for its Nov. 18 hearing, read on Nov. 21, does not list Mr. Gaiser; see the timeline.) Sen. Durbin's remarks about motive are his opinion, and Republicans reject them. Reuters counted 23 nominees still pending on Nov. 15; the Associated Press, citing the chairman's committee, reported 24; The New York Times reported that aides put the number at up to 25.

  2. Exhibit B

    The Answer

    Chairman Chuck Grassley said Democrats had made clear they plan to block President Trump's judges next year, and that he is working to confirm them while there is still time. He said this is no different from how Democrats fast-tracked nominees in recent lame-duck sessions. He pointed to 2022, when he said Democrats held shortened hearings, and to 2024, when, in his words, Democrats "rammed ahead with confirming twenty-two federal judges during lame duck." On background checks, he said nominees may sit for a hearing before their FBI check is cleared, but none will get a committee vote until it is. In comments reported by The New York Times, he said: "The chairman has always had discretion to set hearings." His reply to Sen. Durbin is quoted from a news broadcast of the hearing; it was not found on the chairman's official YouTube channel, so it is not shown in the video.

  3. Exhibit C

    The Precedent

    After President Biden's party lost the White House and the Senate on Nov. 5, 2024, the Democratic Senate confirmed 21 district judges and one appeals court judge before that Congress ended: 22, the chairman's number. On Nov. 13, 2024, Sen. Durbin, then the committee's chairman, gave a floor speech his office titled "We must confirm every possible federal judge in the lame duck session." That push ran into a deal: Democratic leaders agreed to drop four appeals court nominees, who Sen. Schumer's office said did not have the votes, and Republicans agreed to stop delaying votes on district judges. In 2020, after President Trump lost, the Republican Senate confirmed 13 more of his district and appeals court judges. What the record does not settle is Sen. Durbin's narrower point: whether past chairmen also shortened the two-week gap, the 28 days and the background-check step. This file has not found a public record of those timelines for 2022 or 2024. At the Nov. 10 business meeting, the chairman said the Senate had confirmed nearly 60 judges this Congress; Reuters puts it at 57 in President Trump's second term so far. The first confirmation of this lame duck came on Nov. 12: Gregory Cook, to a federal trial court in Alabama.

  4. Exhibit D

    The Hearing Itself

    Five nominees appeared at the Nov. 10 hearing, four of them for judgeships. Sen. John Kennedy questioned Todd Butler, nominated to the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals, about his record defending police officers and public officials in civil rights suits, and asked where the Constitution gives courts the power to strike down laws; Mr. Butler named Marbury v. Madison, the 1803 case that established it. Sen. Durbin asked Mr. Butler about one of those cases; the description of that case and of the court's ruling is Sen. Durbin's, and this file has not checked the court record. Mr. Butler said much of his practice is training officers and officials to respect civil rights, and that when his clients are sued he defends them as well as he can. The chairman asked Mr. Butler about his judicial philosophy. Sen. Richard Blumenthal asked who won the popular vote in the 2020 election; the official recording available to this file ends with the question, so Mr. Butler's answer is not in this file. Republicans on the committee say the nominees are qualified.

  5. Exhibit E

    The Brakes

    Republicans hold the committee 12 to 10. If every Democrat votes no, one Republican no vote makes a tie, and a tie does not advance a nominee. Two Republicans on the committee leave the Senate in January: Thom Tillis of North Carolina is retiring, and John Cornyn of Texas lost his primary. According to Reuters, Sen. Cornyn has declined to commit to supporting Elliot Gaiser, and without his blue slip Mr. Gaiser cannot appear at the committee's next hearing; Sen. Cornyn told the Associated Press there is "a better choice" among other nominees. Sen. Tillis has said he will not move a nominee if one or more of the home-state Republican senators asks him not to, according to Reuters. The chairman defended Oklahoma nominee Zach West against a rating from the American Bar Association in which, the chairman said, a minority of the association's committee found him not qualified; he called the association's ratings "plainly tainted by political bias." The association says its ratings weigh integrity, professional competence and judicial temperament, not a nominee's politics. Reuters reports that Mr. West does not have the votes to clear the committee. A White House official told Reuters it expects every pending judicial nominee to be confirmed this year.

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.

The timeline

  1. Nov. 3, 2020President Trump loses the election. The Republican Senate goes on to confirm 13 more of his district and appeals court judges before that Congress ends.
  2. Nov. 5, 2024Democrats lose the White House and the Senate. The Democratic Senate goes on to confirm 22 judges in the lame duck.
  3. Nov. 13, 2024Sen. Durbin, then the committee's chairman, gives a floor speech his office titles "We must confirm every possible federal judge in the lame duck session."
  4. Nov. 3, 2026Democrats win control of the Senate.
  5. Nov. 10, 2026Senate Judiciary Committee business meeting and nominations hearing; Sen. Durbin and Chairman Grassley dispute the committee's practices.
  6. Nov. 12, 2026First confirmation of the lame duck: Gregory Cook, to a federal trial court in Alabama.
  7. Nov. 17, 2026Update: The committee voted to send three district court nominees to the full Senate. Robert E. Long Jr. (Florida) and Jesus A. Osete (Missouri) advanced 12-10, with every Republican voting yes and every Democrat no; Peter M. Mansfield (Louisiana) advanced 14-8. The two Oklahoma district court nominees, Zachary Paul West and Trevor Scott Pemberton, were held over under Committee Rule I.3, as were a U.S. marshal nominee and one bill, according to the committee's published results.
  8. Nov. 18, 2026Update: The committee's page for its Nov. 18 nominations hearing listed James Cody Hiland for the Eighth Circuit and six district court nominees, for North Carolina (two), Nebraska, South Carolina, Indiana and Texas. Elliot Gaiser was not on the list as read on Nov. 21; the page gives no reason.
  9. Nov. 21, 2026Update: As of Nov. 21, the committee's website lists its Nov. 24 business meeting as canceled; the page does not say when or why. The agenda on the page includes Mr. West and Mr. Pemberton. We had planned to post an update within 48 hours of the Nov. 18 hearing. The three updates dated Nov. 17, Nov. 18 and Nov. 21 were all posted on Nov. 21, after that window.
  10. NextThe new Senate takes office on Jan. 3, 2027. This file stays open: It will read the confirmation votes against what was said on Nov. 10.

Open questions

  1. If a norm is set aside each time a majority is about to lose power, is it still a norm?
  2. The blue slip gives a single senator a say over a lifetime appointment. Which way will each side argue about it in January?
  3. Is either party willing to agree on a rule now, before it knows who it will be used against?
