Case File #013

Kennedy on Drug Prices: "It's the Intermediaries" and Six Bills

On Tuesday, Nov. 17, 2026, the Senate Judiciary Committee held a hearing titled "Prescribing Sunshine: How Competition & Transparency Lowers Prescription Drug Costs." Sen. John Kennedy (R-La.) said of the market for prescription drugs: "It's the intermediaries." The intermediaries are pharmacy benefit managers, or PBMs, which set prices, decide which drugs a plan covers, process claims and build pharmacy networks. In its July 2024 interim staff report, the Federal Trade Commission's staff found that the three largest PBMs handled nearly 80 percent of prescriptions in 2023 and the six largest about 94 percent; the study is not finished. Five witnesses testified: a patient advocate, the co-founder of an online pharmacy that competes with the pharmacies the big PBMs own, the head of the Iowa Pharmacy Association, a health economist and the chief executive of a Chicago health center. No PBM, insurer or drug manufacturer testified. The committee chairman, Sen. Chuck Grassley (R-Iowa), kept returning to six bills the committee approved by voice vote on April 3, 2025. Five deal with patents and generic competition; one would require the FTC to finish its PBM study. One, the Affordable Prescriptions for Patients Act (S. 1041), passed the Senate by unanimous consent on July 21, 2026; it has not become law, and the House has not acted. The other five have been on the Senate calendar since April 10, 2025, and congress.gov shows no floor action. Kennedy said "I am embarrassed that my side has been in control for two years, and we've done nothing"; the chairman answered that the committee has marked up six bills. This file does not rule on who is to blame: it sets out what was said, what the documents say, and what is still open.

Subject
The Senate Judiciary Committee's Nov. 17, 2026 hearing on prescription drug prices, read from the official Senate recording, with each senator's and witness's words in their own voices, set beside the Federal Trade Commission's staff reports, the Medicare price document from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, and the congress.gov record of the six bills the committee approved in April 2025. The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    The Middlemen

    The chairman, Sen. Chuck Grassley (R-Iowa), opened by walking through the supply chain: manufacturers set list prices; wholesalers and distributors move the product; pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) set prices, decide which drugs a plan covers, process claims and build pharmacy networks; insurers and employers design the benefits; pharmacies fill the prescriptions. He said that when the transactions are hidden behind confidential contracts, rebates, fees and payments to affiliated companies, patients and consumers have a hard time "following the money," and that PBMs are "still too opaque and too powerful." In July 2024 the Federal Trade Commission's staff released an interim report on PBMs. By its count, the three largest PBMs handled nearly 80 percent of prescriptions in 2023, and the six largest about 94 percent. Those are the staff's findings; the study is not finished. Five witnesses testified. One was Dr. Alex Oshmyansky, co-founder and chief executive of the Mark Cuban Cost Plus Drug Company, an online pharmacy that competes with the pharmacies the big PBMs own; he was describing his competitors. He said that the top three things driving high drug costs are "monopolies, monopolies, monopolies," and compared PBMs that own pharmacies to this: "It's like hiring somebody to negotiate the price of a used car for you, but that person also owns the car lot." Prof. Neeraj Sood, a health economist at the University of Southern California, gave a made-up example of two drugs that work equally well; the numbers are his illustration, not real prices. Drug A has a list price of $1,000 and a rebate of $650, so its net price is $350; the patient pays about 20 percent of the list price, $200, and the plan pays $150. Drug B has a list price of $400 and a rebate of $100, so its net price is $300; the patient pays $80 and the plan pays $220. Plans prefer drug A, he said, while patients and the health care system prefer drug B; "a PBM working in the best interest of a plan would choose drug A and increase costs for patients." No PBM, insurer or drug manufacturer testified, so the PBMs' case reached the room only secondhand. Prof. Sood said PBMs say they negotiate hard and manage benefits efficiently, and that those decisions should come with accountability to patients. The chairman said PBMs would argue they are getting the best deal for their clients, and that their lack of openness makes him question that position. Sen. Dick Durbin (D-Ill.), the committee's ranking member, said: "If you read a PBM defense of what they're doing, it sounds perfectly reasonable." The "four times" price figure he went on to cite is his; we did not check it.

  2. Exhibit B

    The Pharmacies

    Sen. Durbin spoke of pharmacy closures in Illinois and linked them to drug costs and declining reimbursement; that link is his statement, and we did not check it. Karriem Watson, chief executive of the UI Health Mile Square Health Center in Chicago, wrote in his testimony that 838 community pharmacies closed in Illinois between 2009 and 2021. Matthew Pitlick, chief executive of the Iowa Pharmacy Association, spoke for Iowa's pharmacists: "Iowa's pharmacists are not asking for protection from competition, we're asking for competition." And: "Sunshine matters because markets cannot discipline prices they cannot see." The Iowa Pharmacy Association represents pharmacists, who have a stake in the rules.

  3. Exhibit C

    One Medicare Price

    Since January 2026, Medicare has paid negotiated prices for ten drugs. Sen. Durbin brought up one of them, the blood thinner Eliquis, as he built an argument for Medicare for All: "Eliquis originally was $521 for a 30-day supply. It's now $231 for a 30-day supply." The government's own numbers match his. According to the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services fact sheet, the 2023 list price for a 30-day supply of Eliquis was $521; the negotiated price for 2026 is $231, a discount of 56 percent. About 3.9 million people in Medicare drug plans used it in 2023. The $231 is the price Medicare's drug plans pay, not what a patient pays at the pharmacy counter; the $521 is a 2023 list price, also not a figure for what a patient pays. Several Republican senators, including the chairman, argued against Medicare for All at the hearing. That debate is outside this file.

  4. Exhibit D

    The Price You Can See

    Sen. Durbin said there are only two countries in the world that allow direct-to-consumer television ads for drugs, the United States and New Zealand. That is his statement; we did not check it. He said he and the chairman put together a bill to require those ads to show the drug's price. The official record shows he introduced it as S. 229, the Drug-price Transparency for Consumers Act, on Jan. 23, 2025, and it was referred to the Senate Finance Committee; no further action is listed. At the pharmacy counter, Prof. Sood said the change that would matter most is about which price your share is based on: "that would be the biggest policy win if we can have patient cost sharing be based on the net price of the drug, not the list price." In his opening statement he also named a trade-off: "If cost sharing is based on net prices, the sickest patients will pay less but premiums will increase."

  5. Exhibit E

    The Drugmakers' Share

    Not everyone in the room put the blame in the same place. Sen. John Kennedy (R-La.) first made the case for the drug companies: "Those who risk their capital to develop these drugs are entitled to recoup their costs and a fair return on their investment." Merith Basey, chief executive of the advocacy group Patients For Affordable Drugs Now, pushed back. She said federal research is behind most new drugs; that claim is hers, and her written testimony puts it more narrowly: federal research support behind more than 99 percent of the drugs approved between 2010 and 2019. She said the system needs its loopholes closed, because they let pharmaceutical corporations extend their monopolies. Sen. Kennedy answered: "I don't think we're disagreeing, I said that. But you're acting as if pharmaceutical drug companies are Darth Vader. They're the ones who develop these drugs. The problem is, as I see it, and they're entitled to a fair return, the problems I see are the intermediaries." Ms. Basey's group calls drugmakers' patent tactics a major driver of high prices. Five of the six committee bills deal with patents and generic competition: patent thickets (S. 1041), product switching (S. 1040), deals that delay generics (S. 1096), petitions that delay generics (S. 1095), and a task force linking the patent office and the Food and Drug Administration (S. 1097). Only one of the six, S. 527, is about PBMs: it would require the Federal Trade Commission to finish its PBM study and send recommendations to Congress. The witnesses themselves differed on who is responsible. This file does not rule on whether drugmakers, PBMs or both set prices too high.

  6. Exhibit F

    The Six Bills

    The Judiciary Committee approved the six bills by voice vote on April 3, 2025. One, the Affordable Prescriptions for Patients Act (S. 1041), passed the full Senate by unanimous consent on July 21, 2026; it has not become law, and the House has not acted on it (held at the desk). The other five (S. 527, S. 1040, S. 1095, S. 1096 and S. 1097) were reported and placed on the Senate calendar on April 10, 2025; congress.gov shows no floor action on them since. Sen. Kennedy, whose party has controlled the Senate for these two years, said: "I am embarrassed that my side has been in control for two years, and we've done nothing. Zero. Zilch. Nada. We haven't even tried to move this legislation." For the record: one of the six did pass the Senate in July; the other five have not had a floor vote. Later, Sen. Peter Welch (D-Vt.) said: "we've got a reset opportunity here and this is not at all a Republican-Democratic deal." The chairman answered: "don't forget we've already voted out six bills out of this committee that would save drug prices ... let's get those pieces of legislation moving." Sen. Ashley Moody (R-Fla.) said the committee has passed out six bipartisan prescription drug competition bills by voice vote, and "these should be bills that should be taken up by the floor and we should get moving." At the end of the hearing the chairman came back to Sen. Kennedy's remark: "I appreciate Senator Kennedy's frustration that he expressed. To set the record straight, this committee has marked up six bills that will lower drug costs." That the bills will lower drug costs is his statement of what they are for; this file does not evaluate it.

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.

The timeline

  1. June 2022The Federal Trade Commission opens a study of pharmacy benefit manager business practices.
  2. July 2024The FTC staff releases its first interim report on PBMs: in 2023 the three largest handled nearly 80 percent of prescriptions and the six largest about 94 percent.
  3. Jan. 2025The FTC releases its second interim staff report, on specialty generic drugs. Sen. Durbin introduces S. 229 (drug prices in TV ads) on Jan. 23; it is referred to the Senate Finance Committee.
  4. Apr. 3, 2025The Senate Judiciary Committee approves six drug-price bills by voice vote: S. 527, S. 1040, S. 1041, S. 1095, S. 1096 and S. 1097.
  5. Apr. 10, 2025The six bills are reported without a written report and placed on the Senate calendar.
  6. Jan. 1, 2026Medicare's negotiated prices for ten drugs, including Eliquis ($231 for a 30-day supply), take effect.
  7. July 21, 2026S. 1041, the Affordable Prescriptions for Patients Act, passes the Senate by unanimous consent; it is held at the House desk.
  8. Oct. 22, 2026Sens. Grassley and Cantwell write that the FTC has yet to complete its study of PBM business practices, opened in June 2022.
  9. Nov. 17, 2026Senate Judiciary Committee hearing "Prescribing Sunshine" with five witnesses; no PBM, insurer or drug manufacturer testifies.

Open questions

  1. Will any of the five bills get a Senate floor vote? As of Nov. 24, 2026, congress.gov shows no floor action since they were placed on the calendar on April 10, 2025. If a bill has not become law when this Congress ends in January, it has to be reintroduced; that goes for all six, including S. 1041.
  2. Will the House act on S. 1041, the one bill that has passed the Senate? As of Nov. 24, 2026, it is held at the desk.
  3. Will the FTC publish its final report on PBMs? Sens. Grassley and Cantwell wrote in October that the commission had yet to complete it; the commission's website lists the two interim reports.
  4. Will the hearing record be published? Senators have one week from the hearing to send written questions; the chairman asked the witnesses to answer within two weeks of receiving them.
  5. When you pick up a prescription, do you know which price your share was based on?
