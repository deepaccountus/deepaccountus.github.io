Case File #012

"A Legal Ponzi Scheme": Johnson, Social Security and the 2032 Date

On Wednesday, Nov. 18, 2026, Sen. Ron Johnson (R-Wis.), chairman of the Senate Budget Committee, opened a hearing by saying: "The title of this hearing is Social Security the Reality, a Legal Ponzi Scheme." Seventy million people received Social Security benefits in December 2025. That morning he had given a television interview; during the hearing, three Democratic senators (Jeff Merkley of Oregon, Mark Warner of Virginia and Ben Ray Luján of New Mexico) read aloud what they said he told the interviewer, and he answered each time. He called his remark "imprecise" and said he is not saying to shut down Social Security; his proposal, he said, is a law so that benefits are not reduced. The Social Security Trustees' 2026 report projects that the retirement (OASI) trust fund's reserves run out in the last quarter of 2032, when incoming money would pay 78 percent of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts; counted together with the disability fund, the date is 2034, at 83 percent. On the day of the hearing the chairman introduced S. 5765, which was sent to the Finance Committee. This file does not rule on the label: it sets out what was said, how the chairman answered, what the Trustees' report says, and what is still open.

Subject
The Senate Budget Committee's Nov. 18, 2026 hearing on Social Security, read from the official Senate recording, with each senator's words and the chairman's answers in his own words, set beside the Social Security Trustees' 2026 report and the chairman's Nov. 17 memo. The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    Read Aloud, and Answered

    On the morning of the hearing the chairman gave a television interview. During the hearing three Democratic senators read aloud what they said he told the interviewer: Sen. Jeff Merkley (D-Ore.), the committee's ranking member, early in the hearing; Sen. Mark Warner (D-Va.); and Sen. Ben Ray Luján (D-N.M.), near the end, who read from a transcript and asked to enter it into the hearing record; the chairman did not object. The three readings do not match word for word, and we have not checked them against the interview itself, so this file presents the remark only as each senator read it. Sen. Merkley said "the biggest factor scaring Americans right now was your quote this morning." The chairman answered: "I'm not saying shut down Social Security. I was talking about Ponzi schemes are shut down. They can't be fixed. So no, my proposal is let's change the law so we don't reduce benefits, stop scaring seniors and then address the overall deficit." Sen. Ron Wyden (D-Ore.) did not read the remark; he described it, and that is his characterization, not a quotation. Through the hearing the chairman repeatedly called his television remark "imprecise" and said his proposal is a law so that benefits are not reduced. His memo, dated the day before the hearing, says: "Once a Ponzi scheme begins to collapse, it can't be fixed. It can only be liquidated ..." And: "Similarly, at this late date, Social Security cannot be fundamentally fixed as a retirement investment fund because it never was one." Those are his words, on paper, before the interview.

  2. Exhibit B

    The Trustees' Numbers

    Every year the Social Security Trustees report on the program's two trust funds; their 2026 report came out in June. Sen. Darline Graham (R-S.C.), appointed in July to the seat of her late brother, Lindsey Graham, cited it at the hearing. What she read matches the report: the retirement and survivors (OASI) trust fund is projected to run out of reserves in the last quarter of 2032; at that point, unless Congress acts, incoming money would pay 78 percent of scheduled benefits. Counted together with the disability fund, the date is 2034, at 83 percent. The disability fund, on its own, is projected to stay funded for the full 75 years. The 78 percent does not hold: the Trustees project it falls gradually to 62 percent by 2100. Seventy-eight percent payable means a cut of about 22 percent. Two witnesses used the same figures: Dr. Veronique de Rugy of the Mercatus Center at George Mason University and Dr. Jason Fichtner of the LIMRA Retirement Income Institute, who said he signed three Trustees reports when he worked at Social Security (witnesses appear as audio only in the video). Sen. Merkley said incoming money would pay less than promised "by an estimated 22 to 28 percent," after citing the Social Security Administration and the Congressional Budget Office for the 2032 date; he did not name a source for the range. The Trustees' figure, for the retirement fund at the moment its reserves run out, is 22; we have not checked the higher number. The report also says the program's total cost has been higher than its total income every year since 2021, and that the retirement fund's date moved one quarter earlier than in last year's report.

  3. Exhibit C

    The Trust Fund

    The chairman showed a photograph as his picture of the trust fund and said: "There is your Social Security trust fund." Sen. Merkley answered that the money is invested, in bonds, and said that "certainly doesn't make it a scam." The Trustees' report says the reserves are held in special issue Treasury securities: $2.56 trillion at the end of 2025, down $160 billion in a year, because the program paid out more than it took in. The funds earned $69 billion in interest that year. Dr. de Rugy told the committee: "Those securities are real legal obligations," and went on to describe how she sees them in budget terms (audio only in the video).

  4. Exhibit D

    The Chairman's Math

    Before the hearing the chairman released a memo with his own calculations. His memo says the data came through the Congressional Research Service; the calculations are his. One asks what would have happened if, after the 1983 reform, the surpluses had gone into a Dow Jones index fund; according to his memo, the answer is stock assets worth approximately $14.5 trillion. We have not verified that figure; it is his calculation. His memo also projects Social Security's deficit in 2032 and draws a conclusion from it; the memo gives no source for the deficit figures (the $511 billion figure is likewise according to his memo, which gives no footnote for it). Dr. Kathryn Anne Edwards of the National Academy of Social Insurance, called by the committee's Democrats, spoke about the debt debate (audio only in the video). The chairman answered that her testimony relies on higher taxes and that, in his view, economic growth comes first.

  5. Exhibit E

    The Label

    Sen. Tim Kaine (D-Va.) began: "I don't want to take the chair's comments out of context. He used the phrase Ponzi scheme today and he's explained here what he meant by that, but he's not the first person." He then asked what the right label is for a program in which working people pay for the retirement of the adults who raised them, and said: "that's why the Ponzi scheme label creates such opposition on this side." The chairman replied: "Obviously, I'm calling it a legal Ponzi scheme to draw attention to the problem," and said of the program, "I'm not suggesting we end it. I'm trying to save it." This file does not rule on which label is right.

  6. Exhibit F

    The Options

    Sen. Graham asked Dr. Fichtner what Congress could do without reducing benefits for people who already depend on them; he answered at length (audio only in the video). The Trustees' report puts the size of the gap in plain terms. To keep the combined program solvent for 75 years starting this year, Congress could raise the payroll tax from 12.4 percent to 16.65 percent, or reduce all benefits by about a quarter, or some mix of the two. If Congress waits until 2034, the report says the changes get larger. The Trustees' own recommendation: lawmakers should address the shortfalls in a timely way, so that changes can be phased in gradually. Sen. Luján said he had heard all three witnesses agree that Social Security should be fixed; Dr. Edwards answered, "A hundred percent." Sen. Warner offered a way to keep fixing the program. The chairman closed the hearing, saying: "I realize I'm making myself a lightning rod here".

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.

The timeline

  3. July 2026Darline Graham (R-S.C.) is appointed to the Senate seat of her late brother, Lindsey Graham.
  4. Nov. 17, 2026Sen. Ron Johnson releases a Budget Committee chairman's memo, "Social Security: The Reality... a Legal Ponzi Scheme."
  5. Nov. 18, 2026Senate Budget Committee hearing "Social Security: The Reality" with three witnesses. The same day Sen. Johnson introduces S. 5765, which is referred to the Finance Committee.

Open questions

  1. Will S. 5765 get cosponsors, a hearing or a vote? As of Nov. 23, 2026, the official record lists it as referred to the Senate Finance Committee, with no cosponsors and no other action.
  2. What will the chairman's December report say? He said he will release a larger report in December, on the federal budget.
  3. Will the hearing record be published? As of Nov. 23, 2026, we have not found the interview transcript Sen. Luján entered, or any written answers from the witnesses, published.
  4. What happens in January? A new Congress convenes; bills not passed by then must start over.
  5. If nothing changes before late 2032, what should come first: more revenue, smaller benefits, or more borrowing?
