Case File #002

The Senators Weren't Told: Jack Smith and Their Phone Toll Records

Jack Smith's office subpoenaed the phone toll records of Republican members of Congress: records of who called whom, not what was said. At prosecutors' request, court orders barred the phone companies from telling them. At a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing on September 29, 2026, senators and Mr. Smith disagreed about why the secrecy was needed and whether it was lawful. The senators' own counts of the members affected do not match.

Subject
The toll-record subpoenas for members of Congress, and the court orders that kept them secret
Exhibits
A through E
Status
Published Nov. 15, 2026

The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    The Accusation

    In his opening statement, Chairman Chuck Grassley made two separate claims. The first is about toll records: he said Mr. Smith sought them for 16 members of Congress. The second is about content: he said Mr. Smith subpoenaed 87 Trump White House phones, knowing they would include communications with members of Congress, 44 members by his count. Sen. Katie Britt, citing press reports, gave a different figure: some 44 subpoenas covering at least 20 Republican members, including at least eight senators. The senators' numbers do not match each other, and this file has not seen the subpoenas themselves. The chairman says his figures come from records and information provided to his committee, some of which he has released.

  2. Exhibit B

    The Answer

    Mr. Smith did not deny obtaining the toll records. He described them as non-content records that show who a call was to, who it was from, and how long it lasted. He said he approved the subpoenas after the Justice Department's Public Integrity Section was consulted, and that the records were materially relevant to the investigation; among other reasons, he said toll records from the evening of January 6 corroborated evidence his office had gathered. Asked by Sen. Dick Durbin where his office found the members' phone numbers, he said he did not recall. On how agents figured out which phones belonged to which senators, he said: "I don't have an answer to that." On the content of messages, Mr. Smith agreed that his office obtained material from the National Archives that contained members' messages; he said it sought the records of White House and government phones, and that members' messages were there because members had been in contact with those phones. The charges in the election case were allegations. Mr. Trump pleaded not guilty, and after the 2024 election the case was dismissed at the special counsel's request, citing Justice Department policy on sitting presidents.

  3. Exhibit C

    The Secrecy Orders

    The subpoenas to the phone companies came with non-disclosure orders. Sen. Britt read the stated grounds, destruction of or tampering with evidence and intimidation of potential witnesses, and asked whether there were reasonable grounds to believe that of the senators on the committee. Mr. Smith answered no and began to say that his concern was that President Trump would act, before he was cut off. He finished the point later, telling Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse that the orders were secured "to prevent anybody, including Donald Trump, from learning about those subpoenas." Sen. Britt, citing records released by the chairman, said the actual reason appeared to be to keep members from challenging the subpoenas under the speech or debate clause. Asked by Sen. John Kennedy and by Sen. Whitehouse, Mr. Smith confirmed that the judges who signed the orders were not told the records belonged to members of Congress; he said that followed department policy at the time.

  4. Exhibit D

    The Law

    The first objection is constitutional. Asked by the chairman what direction he gave his staff on the speech or debate clause, Mr. Smith said his office took its obligations seriously and consulted the Public Integrity Section when it was appropriate or required. The chairman did not accept that answer; he said the documentary record shows that Mr. Smith breached constitutional limits. The second objection is a statute, 2 U.S.C. § 6628, raised by Sen. Ted Cruz. Since 2020 it has said that the Senate's Sergeant at Arms, or a provider used by a Senate office, cannot be barred by a court order from telling that office about legal process seeking its data. Sen. Cruz says the statute made these orders illegal. Mr. Smith said he did not recall the statute being discussed at the time or being part of the application to the judge, and that the orders were consistent with the law and department policy. Whether the statute reaches a senator's records held by an ordinary phone carrier, including a personal phone, is not settled by any court ruling this file has found. Sen. Cruz's questioning is described from his office's recording, which is linked in the sources; it is not shown in the video.

  5. Exhibit E

    The Comparable

    Sen. Whitehouse compared the subpoenas with leak investigations carried out during the first Trump administration. In December 2024, the Justice Department's inspector general reported that in investigations between 2017 and 2020 the department obtained records of two members of Congress, both Democrats, and of 43 congressional staff members from both parties, along with non-disclosure orders. The inspector general found no evidence of retaliatory or partisan motive by the career prosecutors involved, and found that the department had no policy at the time that clearly addressed such requests. Sen. Whitehouse also quoted Sen. Grassley's response at the time: investigations into members of Congress and staff are nothing new. Sen. Grassley's statement said that was especially true for classified leaks, and also that politically motivated probes are a serious offense. Republicans answer that the scale this time was different: by Sen. Cruz's count, the subpoenas sought the phone records of more than a fifth of Senate Republicans.

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.

The timeline

  1. 2017 to 2020Leak investigations in which the Justice Department obtained records of two members of Congress, both Democrats, and 43 congressional staff members from both parties, along with non-disclosure orders. The inspector general later reviewed them.
  2. Dec. 27, 2020Congress amends 2 U.S.C. § 6628: the Senate Sergeant at Arms, and providers serving a Senate office, cannot be barred by a court order from notifying that office of legal process seeking its data.
  3. November 2022Jack Smith is appointed special counsel.
  4. Dec. 10, 2024The Justice Department's inspector general releases its report on the 2017 to 2020 leak investigations.
  5. Sept. 29, 2026Jack Smith testifies before the Senate Judiciary Committee about the toll-record subpoenas.
  6. NextThe chairman says he has released records and will release more. As the documents are released, this file will read them against the testimony.

Open questions

  1. Should prosecutors have to tell a judge when the records they want kept secret belong to members of Congress, no matter which party runs the Justice Department?
  2. The speech or debate clause protects lawmakers from being questioned elsewhere about their legislative work. Does a list of who a senator called, without a word of what was said, cross that line?
  3. Both parties have now been on the receiving end of this method. What rule would each of them accept if they knew the other side would be the one using it?
