Case File #016

SEC Nominee on 401(k)s: "Absolutely Zero Allocation" to Alternatives

On Thursday, Nov. 19, 2026, the Senate Banking Committee, chaired by Sen. Tim Scott (R-S.C.), heard from five nominees, including two for the Securities and Exchange Commission: Steven Levine and Kristin Johnson, a law professor. The SEC has five commissioners by law, no more than three from one party; its website lists two, Chairman Paul Atkins and Commissioner Mark Uyeda. Levine would take the seat last held by Republican Hester Peirce, and Johnson the seat last held by Democrat Caroline Crenshaw. Levine said, under oath, that retirement savers in private 401(k) plans have "absolutely zero allocation" to alternative assets. A 2025 executive order says every American preparing for retirement "should have access to funds" that include alternative assets when the plan's fiduciary decides that access is appropriate; the Labor Department proposed a rule with a "safe harbor" on Mar. 31, 2026, and as of Nov. 27 we found no final rule. Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-Mass.) asked four financial nominees whether private credit could pose serious risk to the financial system; the answers differed. The SEC's own releases show 583 enforcement actions in fiscal 2024 and 348 in fiscal 2026, with money ordered down from $8.2 billion to about $1.1 billion; single large cases swing those totals, and money returned to investors rose from about $262 million to about $731 million between fiscal 2025 and 2026. Senators also raised scams aimed at older savers. As of Nov. 27, the official nomination records list the latest action as "Hearings held," we found no committee vote scheduled, and one of the five SEC seats has no nominee.

The evidence

Exhibits

  1. Exhibit A

    The empty chairs

    By law the SEC has five commissioners, no more than three from one party, and members of different parties "shall be appointed alternately as nearly as may be practicable" (15 U.S.C. 78d). The SEC's website lists two sitting commissioners, Chairman Paul Atkins and Commissioner Mark Uyeda, both Republicans. The White House sent the nominations of Steven Levine and Kristin Johnson to the Senate on Nov. 12, 2026. Levine would take the seat last held by Republican Hester Peirce, for a term ending in June 2030; Johnson, the seat last held by Democrat Caroline Crenshaw, for a term ending in June 2029. Sen. Raphael Warnock (D-Ga.), who introduced Johnson, did the arithmetic on the record. Johnson served on the Commodity Futures Trading Commission from 2022 until September 2025 and told Sen. Angela Alsobrooks (D-Md.) she would push for "a full complement of five commissioners"; Sen. Jack Reed (D-R.I.) said he would work to fill the last Democratic seat "as quickly as possible next year." Levine described himself as a career staff attorney at the SEC who spent the past two years detailed to the White House Council of Economic Advisers and the Labor Department.

  2. Exhibit B

    The retirement door

    On Aug. 7, 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order 14330, "Democratizing Access to Alternative Assets for 401(k) Investors." It says every American preparing for retirement "should have access to funds" that include alternative assets when the plan's fiduciary decides that access is appropriate; it does not require any plan to offer them. It told the Labor Department to clarify its position within 180 days, weighing "potentially higher expenses" against the goal of better long-term returns, and asked the SEC to "consider ways" to make access easier. The Labor Department withdrew a 2021 statement urging caution about private equity in 401(k) plans on Aug. 12, 2025, and on Mar. 31, 2026 proposed a rule with a "safe harbor" for fiduciaries; as of Nov. 27, 2026, we found no final rule in the Federal Register. Chairman Scott asked how to give everyday investors more choices and lower costs; Levine said a vote on the accredited-investor definition could come before him, so he would not prejudge it, but that it "seems completely ridiculous" that someone earning $199,000 a year has no access to certain investments while someone earning a little more does. Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-Ohio) asked whether everyday Americans lack access to certain private funds; Levine answered "That's correct." Warren, in her opening statement, saw the order differently.

  3. Exhibit C

    The warning, and the answers

    Warren called private credit "lightly regulated" and said Moody's found banks have $300 billion of exposure to it; she also said Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had compared the risks in private credit to the 2008 subprime mortgage crisis. Those are her figures and characterizations; we did not check them. She asked the four financial nominees for a yes or no on whether private credit could pose serious risk to the financial system. Hugo Dante Jr., nominee to lead the Treasury's Office of Financial Research, and Rebekah Jurata, nominee for Assistant Secretary of the Treasury for Financial Institutions, answered first. Levine said any asset could potentially pose a risk depending on concentration and that he had seen no evidence that private credit currently poses a risk here; Johnson said she is a systemic risk scholar and believes there is the potential for systemic risk concerns in that market. Sen. Mike Rounds (R-S.D.) gave Dante time to finish his answer; the figures he gave are his, drawn, he said, from bank reports to regulators. Jurata said private credit provides credit to many small businesses. Levine said the Dodd-Frank law requires the SEC to collect data on private funds, including private credit funds, and that the form is still in use. Johnson said her answer reflected research by economists and regulators since the financial crisis.

  4. Exhibit D

    The numbers

    On Nov. 10, 2026, the SEC published its enforcement results for fiscal year 2026. Warren asked Levine about them; he said he could not speak to the breakdown but knew the results released this year are lower in total than in previous years. The SEC's own yearly releases show 583 enforcement actions in fiscal 2024, 456 in 2025 and 348 in 2026, a drop of about 40 percent from 2024 to 2026, in line with Warren's first figure; she did not say which years she compared. Money ordered fell from $8.2 billion in fiscal 2024 to about $1.1 billion in 2026, about 87 percent. Two cautions: single big cases swing these totals (more than half of the 2024 amount came from one judgment against Terraform Labs and its founder; the 2025 total, nearly $18 billion, was swollen by judgments in the old Stanford Ponzi scheme case), and money ordered is not money paid back: the amount the SEC returned to harmed investors rose from about $262 million in 2025 to about $731 million in 2026, sent to more than 250,000 investors, and awards to whistleblowers rose from about $60 million to more than $158 million. Warren's figure for cases against public companies, down 80 percent, is not broken out in the SEC's release; we could not check it. Reed's historical comparisons are his; we did not find them in the SEC's release. Johnson and Levine both used the phrase "cop on the beat"; Levine said he could not speak to the past couple of years because he had been on detail elsewhere, and that the Division of Enforcement would have his support. Asked by Warnock whether fraud itself had gone down, Levine said he had not seen evidence "one way or another."

  5. Exhibit E

    The scams

    Republican senators led on scams aimed at ordinary savers. Chairman Scott told Treasury nominee Rebekah Jurata that Americans are facing "the highest levels of scams and fraud" in a long time. Sen. Bill Hagerty (R-Tenn.) asked Jurata what the Treasury can do and said the problem is escalating in his state, "this elder scam type of thing." The SEC's own release describes an alleged offering fraud whose investors included people, in its words, "many of whom were retired senior citizens," and a new working group on fraud against retail investors. In her opening statement, Johnson spoke about schemes that harm vulnerable investors, so vulnerable that a single market shock can erase years of savings.

Charges in an indictment are allegations, not findings of guilt. Statements by senators and officials are reported as their statements.
